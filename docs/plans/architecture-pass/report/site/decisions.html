Where the qualities pull against each other

The quality targets on Requirements fall under twelve qualities. In the top-level design some of them pull against each other. The requirements already answer most of those conflicts: some rest on your decisions, some on targets still marked proposed or partly settled, which you can flag in the review below; the panel designs to them as they stand:

The designer's iteration speed against the promise “Each lifecycle button does exactly what its name says”. A change reaches a game only between games, or the engine refuses and says why (A published change plays within minutes, Change one value between shows and play it).

The designer's iteration speed against safe release and rollback. Packs publish at any time and the engine changes only between events (Replugging the old machine undoes a release); a pack that needs an ability this engine lacks is refused at publish with the ability named (A broken pack is refused at the desk); abilities are only ever added (New abilities for a second game break nothing).

Standalone and offline against easy to understand and change. A standalone tablet is a second decider in the code, but there is one decider per game (Standalone games: declared, never mixed). Whether the engine and the tablet run one body of rules or two kept equal by tests is a later choice for the design rounds.

Privacy and access against staying up through a live show. Passes survive a reconnect, a restart and a machine swap, and nobody logs in again mid-show (Every request is checked against its pass, A tablet reconnects to the same facts, Replugging the old machine undoes a release).

Privacy and access against the promise that the night can be told afterwards. Game data stays on the kit; you move the record, and the director's console stays a separate program outside the engine (The night's record and the after-game article, Game data stays on the kit).

Operating without technical help against privacy and access. The kit holds its own passes, the GM logs in once a night, and staff who log in hold the game's controls (Power on straight into a running show, Every request is checked against its pass).

Running well at the size of a show against more than one copy, kit or pack. A few dozen devices is the setting the panel gets (What the architects' panel sees, and when), and phones are measured before the first is admitted (Phone capacity is measured before the first phone).

Testable without the live show against easy to extend. Every added program or device kind comes with a software stand-in and its tests (A different lighting system or video player, Every test runs twice and never skips silently).

Every screen telling the truth against standalone and offline. A standalone game says standalone, and every service the engine runs reads not installed tonight, grey (A standalone game is normal, never a fault).

Seven conflicts stay open, and six questions below answer them: two conflicts meet in “The show machine fails mid-game”; one, easy to extend against easy to understand and change, gives two questions, “What a game's maker may build inside the pack” and “How an extension for all games reaches a production”; and one, the standing rule that phones and hardware scanners stay equal in ability, against standalone and offline, sits with the standalone games below. A. A GM tablet cut off from the engine

What it decides

Staying up through a live show against every screen telling the truth. What a GM tablet does with a scan while it cannot reach the engine for a short time, a dropped wifi or an engine restart. What the GM may tell the team at that moment. What happens when the connection returns and the engine disagrees with what the tablet showed. The same answer holds for the first minutes of a longer failure, before the GMs know whether the show machine is coming back.

What it means in practice
  • Your decision settles that the game goes on (No technical failure stops a game), and the quality target A refused scan never reads as complete proposes that a refused scan never reads as complete; you settled that only for a standalone game, and the networked part is proposed. Open: what the tablet shows meanwhile, and who wins a conflict.
  • At a show. The tablet's wifi drops for a minute. A team hands the GM a token. Today the tablet shows “Transaction Complete” with points, from its own copy of the rules, and keeps the scan to send to the engine later (Tablets and phones decide facts the engine owns). If another tablet sold the same token during that minute, the engine refuses the kept scan on reconnect, and the GM has already told the team it earned money. The quality target A tablet reconnects to the same facts asks that the game carries on and that the GM can always see what became of such a scan, and leaves what the tablet does with it meanwhile, and who wins a conflict, to this question.
  • For the GM at the failure. With a refusal the GM says “give me a minute, the connection is down” and the line waits. With a kept scan the GM says “it is in, your result shows when we are back”. With a provisional verdict the GM says “you earned fifty thousand” and may have to take it back. Your words are that pausing a game for a lost connection is the worst scenario, and that the GMs need to know their fallbacks. The question is what the fallback shows.
  • For the designer. Nothing changes in the pack. A designer of a game with group bonuses should know that a provisional bonus can be undone.
  • For a production adopting the engine. A venue with worse wifi than yours makes this the common case. A production with one GM tablet can never have a conflict, so for them a kept scan and a provisional verdict come to the same thing. A production with four tablets at four tables has conflicts every time the wifi drops.
  • Under the two options that keep the scan, you also set the conflict rule: when two tablets both sold one token during the gap, who wins. The earlier by the tablets' clocks (clocks differ). The first to reach the engine (luck). Or the GM decides from a red row and tells the losing team. This is a choice between fairness by time and simplicity.
  • Which decision ahead depends on it. “Where authority lives across devices, offline and standalone included” names this question in what it decides. Refusal means the engine is the only decider and tablets are screens. Keep-and-label means the protocol has a provisional state and the engine orders scans by their time. Provisional verdicts mean the rules run in two places and a reconciliation exists. “The published protocol's form” follows: what every reply carries (how sure the engine is) depends on whether provisional replies exist. “How the engine holds the game's state” follows too, since kept scans must be ordered against the engine's record.

The options

  • Refuse while cut off.

    What it means The GM sees a plain reason and nothing is kept; teams wait. The engine is the only decider, the tablet holds no game fact of its own, and the protocol needs no “not yet decided” state.

    What it costs Every wifi drop costs show time, which is close to the pause you called the worst scenario.

  • Keep and label.

    What it means The tablet records the scan as kept and not yet decided, shows no points, and shows the GM a list of kept scans. On reconnect the engine decides each in order and the tablet shows the final answer; a kept scan refused later becomes a red row the GM must act on. GMs learn one habit: “in, pending”.

    What it costs The protocol carries a provisional state in replies and in the tablet's list, and the engine must order kept scans by when they happened, which meets the finding of the two clocks.

  • Keep and decide provisionally under the shared rules.

    What it means The tablet applies the same rules the engine would, from its last known copy of the game, and shows the verdict and the points marked provisional. The engine confirms or overturns on reconnect. The richest fallback for the GM.

    What it costs The game's rules must run equally on the engine and the tablet; every fact the tablet shows must carry “provisional” in the protocol; a conflict rule and a conflict screen exist. The most to keep equal across two places, for years.

Recommendation

Keep and label. It keeps the game going, as you decided, and it keeps the tablet from telling a team something the engine has not decided, which A refused scan never reads as complete proposes for a networked game and your words on fallbacks support. It costs a provisional state in the protocol and one habit for the GMs. What we need from you beyond that is the conflict rule: when two tablets both sold one token while apart, the earlier by the tablets' clocks, the first to reach the engine, or the GM decides from a red row. That is a choice between fairness by time and simplicity, and the design cannot make it for you.

B. The show machine fails mid-game

What it decides

Staying up through a live show against standalone and offline, and against every screen telling the truth on the public screens. What a networked game does when the show machine stops and does not come back for the rest of the night. What fallbacks the GMs must have in front of them at that moment. What the players may lose, and what they must never lose. How the results come back together afterwards. Whether a game may change from networked to standalone while running, and what that does to Standalone games: declared, never mixed.

What it means in practice
  • Settled: no game mixes networked and standalone devices, and the game must go on (Standalone games: declared, never mixed, No technical failure stops a game). Settled for the players' own devices: a scanner shows the player that a scan is kept and not yet sent, and never promises a video it cannot start (Offline scans are kept and sent once). Open: how the game goes on, and what the audience's screens do.
  • At a show. Fifty minutes in, the show machine's power supply dies. The TV freezes on the scoreboard. The music stops. The players' phones and the hardware scanners stop sending; the hardware scanner keeps each scan on its card, shows a red Disconnected, and for a video token says rescan later, which cannot work; the phones say offline. The GM tablets lose the engine. Today each tablet has its own copy of the game as of the last message and keeps scanning with its own verdict. Nothing merges two tablets' records afterwards, and no spare takes over.
  • For the GM at the failure. Your words: the GMs need to know their available fallbacks. The tablet should show them as a red row with actions, the pattern Every red row carries its own actions settles. Which actions exist is this question. “Carry on from this tablet.” “Switch to the spare.” “End the game from here.” Each is a different engine.
  • For the designer. Cues stop with the machine. The designer must know which effects die with it: video, music, lights, the TV, the game clock. That is the same list a standalone game gives up (the question “What a standalone game gives up, and what it keeps”).
  • For a production adopting the engine. A production with no spare machine. A production whose show is mostly the TV, for whom carrying on means little. A production that runs two games a night and cannot afford a lost one. Each would choose differently; the engine must say which fallbacks it offers.
  • For the players. You asked for as little effect on the player experience as possible. The design cannot promise the TV and the videos without a second machine. You must say what the players may lose for the rest of a night (video, music, the public evidence screen) and what they must never lose (their scans counted exactly once, no team paid twice for one token). On the public screens there is a second choice: when the engine goes silent, Screens say when the engine goes silent asks the TV to stop its clock and show it as unconfirmed, which is honest and visible; the alternative is a TV that keeps counting by itself, which is smooth and may be wrong.
  • Which decision ahead depends on it. “How the engine holds the game's state”, the first and hardest to reverse. A spare that takes over live needs a record that can be copied as it changes; carrying on from tablets needs tablets that hold enough of the record to finish a game; a single store on one machine serves neither. “Where authority lives across devices” follows: whether a tablet or a spare may become the decider, and when. “How one engine runs as many separate copies” follows: a standby is a copy of the engine with the show's identity. “The published protocol's form” follows: how a device finds the engine again under a new machine, and whether a pass issued by one machine holds on another.

The options

  • Carry on from the tablets and reconcile afterwards.

    What it means Each GM tablet keeps taking scans, as kept or as provisional under the question above. The GMs end the game from the tablets; the night's record is assembled from their records with the conflict rule. The player devices keep their scans and reach the record only when a machine comes back, or through a collection step after the show. The players lose video, music and the TV for the rest of the night.

    What it costs Every tablet must hold enough of the game to finish it (the teams, the claims, the scores, the pack it ran), and a merge exists for several tablets. The cheapest kit, and a recovery the GMs must rehearse.

  • A spare machine takes over at once.

    What it means A second show machine, or a tablet running an engine of its own, holds a live copy of the game's record, takes the engine's name on the kit's own network when the first falls silent, and every device finds it again, the way Replugging the old machine undoes a release already asks devices to carry on when the old machine is replugged. The players lose a minute.

    What it costs The engine's record must be copied live as it changes (a numbered log of facts), the kit's name must move between machines, and passes must hold on both. The most to build and to own (a second machine in every kit), and the strongest promise.

  • A cold spare from the last saved record.

    What it means A second machine or a tablet starts the engine from the latest saved copy of the game, kept every few seconds on the tablets or on a stick. The GM starts it by hand; the tablets re-send what they kept since the copy.

    What it costs Between the two above in cost and in the players' loss.

  • Declare the game standalone once, for the rest of the night.

    What it means One GM tablet becomes the decider from its last copy of the game; the other devices join it (which needs a channel between devices, in effect a small engine on the tablet) or stop. This would amend Standalone games: declared, never mixed: a game's declaration could change once, one way, for the whole game, with every device told. It keeps “never mixed”, since after the change no device is on an engine.

    What it costs An amendment to your decision, which only you can make; a small engine on the tablet.

  • Pause until the machine returns.

    What it means Listed only to say it is out. Your words call it the worst possible scenario.

    What it costs The show stops.

What we need from you

From you, in one line: which of the first three fallbacks the engine must offer (one or more); whether the fourth, declaring the game standalone once for the rest of the night, may be offered as well (yes or no), which amends your decision on standalone games only if you say yes; and whether the TV may keep counting while the engine is silent (yes or no). What the players may lose follows from the fallback you pick; add to it only if you want more protected than their scans counted once.

C. Release day: what must update together, and how long older devices work

What it decides

Safe release and rollback against easy to extend and operating without technical help. Whether a new version of the engine requires every device and pack to be at the matching version (everything updates together), or the engine must keep working with older GM tablets, phones, hardware scanners and packs for a stated time. How long that time is. Whether a mismatch refuses the device or warns the GM. Whom the promise is for: your kit, or every adopter's.

What it means in practice
  • You said yes, generally, to everything updating together, and asked what the question means in practice and how it affects the design and the build, especially in the long term once the new engine runs ALN. The explanation follows. Settled already: the show machine keeps one written list of what it should carry and names any piece that differs (The machine matches what it should carry); replugging the old machine undoes a release (Replugging the old machine undoes a release). Open: what happens to the piece that differs, and for how long an older piece works.
  • What the two phrases mean. “Everything updates together” means one release moves the engine, the GM tablet's app, the phones' page, the hardware scanners' firmware and the protocol between them, and the engine refuses anything at another version. “Older versions keep working with a newer engine” means the engine accepts a device or a pack from an earlier release for a stated time, and the protocol is built so that it can.
  • Today, the GM tablet's app. The engine serves it, so a tablet that reloads after the engine is updated gets the new app. But a tablet that was open before the update, or one holding the app in its browser's cache, keeps running the old app against the new engine until it reloads. At connection the tablet sends its app version and the fingerprint of the pack it loaded; the engine records the version, uses it only to label the station in its list, and writes a pack mismatch to its log only (Pack edits reach each copy of the pack differently). The list of messages the tablet listens for is kept by hand in the tablet's code; a message the engine adds and the tablet does not know never arrives, with no error (Names joining two places are kept by hand).
  • Today, the phones' page and the hardware scanners. In a networked game the engine serves the phones' page too, from a copy placed in its public folder; the page caches itself under a name that must be changed by hand to make phones refresh. The hardware scanners' firmware changes only by cable; the board leaves no room for an update over the air. Their token list, pictures and sounds sync at boot from the engine. So a hardware scanner is the device most likely to be older than the engine, and a change to the token file it reads breaks every scanner that was not flashed.
  • Today, packs and the protocol. Packs are published separately, at any time. The engine refuses a pack whose format version does not match exactly, a pack that requires an ability the engine lacks, and a pack that names a minimum engine version the engine does not meet; the GM tablet applies only the exact format check. The protocol is two written lists (web requests and live messages), the pack's formats and the venue profile's format, checked by a few tests, and every program copies the names by hand. So today nothing enforces “together” and nothing promises “older works”. Mixtures happen silently: a rollback can leave the machine running pieces from two releases (The deployed game is many parts nothing compares), and a tablet at the wrong version is a log line.
  • In the long term, for your kit. Once the new engine runs ALN, your kit updates between events (Replugging the old machine undoes a release): build the new machine, check it against one written list of what it should carry (The machine matches what it should carry), rehearse, swap by replugging (A new engine version by swapping machines). The pieces that can still lag behind the engine on a show night are the hardware scanners (cable), tablets that did not reload, the standalone phone page's own copy of the pack (which changes only when the page is republished), and packs published later than the engine. The published protocol is what makes each of these either refused or tolerated.
  • In the long term, for adopters. Other productions have their own release pace, their own fleets of cable-updated devices, and perhaps add-ons or their own builds (the question “How an extension for all games reaches a production”). They build against the protocol and the pack format (One published protocol and an outside test bench). For them, a protocol that may change shape at every release with no window is a reason not to adopt, or to copy and freeze. A protocol with a window is a promise you must keep for years.
  • At a show, under each answer. A hardware scanner missed its cable update and connects to the new engine. Under everything-together the engine refuses it, the GM's tablet names it, and the scanner is useless tonight unless someone has a cable and a laptop. Under a window it works tonight and the GM's tablet shows “older firmware, update before the next show”. Packs already have their rule and it does not change here: a pack that needs an ability this engine lacks is refused at publish with the ability named (A broken pack is refused at the desk), and abilities are only ever added (New abilities for a second game break nothing), so an old pack runs on a new engine.
  • How the answer changes the protocol and the build. Under everything-together the protocol has one version number for the whole release; messages may change shape freely between releases, since no two versions ever meet; code on every side is generated from one definition per release; the test bench tests one combination; and the hardware scanners must be at the release before the show, which means either a cable step on the pre-show checklist at every release or a firmware update over the kit's network at boot, which the design rounds would have to add and which has its own cost (room on the scanner for two copies of its program, a fallback that cannot leave the board unable to start). Under a window every message carries its version; inside the window changes are additive (fields are added, never removed or renamed, and a device ignores what it does not know); the engine accepts the current version and a stated number of earlier ones and tells the GM which devices are behind; the test bench runs the engine against each supported device version, a set of combinations that grows with the window; and a written rule says when a version is dropped.
  • Which decision ahead depends on it. “The published protocol's form” says in what it decides that it settles the version rules; the item above is what each answer does to them. “How today's engine moves to the new design” follows in part: the swap from the show machine in use now to the new show machine is itself a release, and whether old devices may meet the new engine during the move depends on this.

The options

  • Everything updates together.

    What it means One version for the release; the engine refuses any device at another version; the tablet and phone apps come from the engine, so they move with it; hardware scanners must be flashed before the show; packs are checked at publish as now.

    What it costs The simplest protocol and one combination to test, and show-night work at every release for every cable-updated device, for you and for every adopter.

  • Together for what the engine serves, a window for the rest.

    What it means The engine forces a stale tablet or phone app to reload, so nothing it serves is ever at another version. Cable-updated devices and packs work for a stated window (one or two engine versions back), and the GM's tablet shows which devices are behind.

    What it costs The hardware scanner's part of the protocol is kept small and changed rarely, with additive changes only inside the window; tests of the engine against each supported scanner version; a rule for when a version is dropped. Cable visits when you choose, not every release.

  • A window for everything.

    What it means Every message carries its version; the engine speaks to older tablets, phones, scanners and packs within the window.

    What it costs A version on every message, the engine and each device agreeing on a version when they connect, more combinations to test and a written rule for when an old version stops working, kept forever. The most freedom for adopters.

Recommendation

Together for what the engine serves, a window for the rest. Your lean is everything together (“Generally speaking, yes”). The agent recommends one exception, for cable-updated devices, as its own view: anyone with a fleet of hardware scanners needs it, and the scanner asks for little (a token list, one scan, a batch of scans, the engine's health, its assets), so keeping its part of the protocol small and stable is cheap. From you: how long the window is (one or two engine versions back), and whether a device outside it is refused or only warned about.

D. What a game's maker may build inside the pack

What it decides

Easy to extend against easy to understand and change, and the standing rule of parity between phones and hardware scanners against standalone and offline. How far a game's own maker may go inside the pack, with no developer. Values in tables, as today: modes by their flags, scoring tables, groups, cues on a clock. Rules the designer writes as “when this happens, do that”, wiring triggers the engine knows to responses the engine knows; your example is players changing the room's lighting by scanning tokens on a player scanner. Or scripts, code the designer writes into the pack. And whether such rules must also run where there is no engine, on a standalone tablet or on a hardware scanner alone, since phones and hardware scanners stay equal in ability and designers change pack data, never device code.

What it means in practice
  • Settled: no word of ALN's inside the engine (No word of ALN's inside the engine); designers change pack data, never device code; a whole game is built without code (A designer builds a whole game without code); parity is in ability, not in screens; hardware scanner work waits on no game. Your decision keeps the two kinds of extension apart and leans, in tentative words, to the game's own maker building the first kind inside the game (Two kinds of game extension). Open: how far the pack goes, and whether pack rules must run on a scanner alone.
  • For the designer. The requirements already put your lighting example in tables: the settled case The pack says what a scan causes, due with players' phones, lets the pack say what a scan causes, and Players change the room's lighting by scanning tokens has the designer write “this token's scan triggers that look” there, with no developer. What tables cannot do is a combination no declaration yet names, such as a scan of a token in this group lighting this role only while the clock is in this phase; each such combination waits for a developer to add a declaration for it. Today a game played only on GM tablets reaches video through one standing cue per token, which is the tables-only workaround until that declaration exists (The pack says what a scan causes). With when-then rules the designer writes any such combination in the design tool and plays it within minutes, which is the loop the standing rules call the point of the engine work. With scripts the designer can do anything, and the check at the desk can promise little.
  • At a show. A rule the designer wrote misfires: every scan flips the lights. The GM must be able to switch that rule off from the tablet, as a standing cue is switched off today, and the check at publish must have refused rules that cannot run (a lighting role the venue profile never bound). A rule that runs on the engine is one more thing the GM's Show Control section lists.
  • For the people maintaining the engine. Tables keep the engine small and readable. A rule format is a second language to version, check, test on two games and document for adopters, and it grows unless someone says no. Scripts need a fenced-off place to run, with limits, and a way to keep a script from harming the show or the kit.
  • For a production adopting the engine. A theatre company with no developer wants to combine abilities themselves. A company with a developer may prefer code and find a rule format limiting. The hardware scanner matters here: a rule “when scanned, play this sound” must run on the device alone in a standalone game, so the scanner's firmware must carry a runner for whatever rules the pack may hold, or the scanner asks the engine and a standalone scanner runs only what a device alone can.
  • Your words add one more thing for later: rules a game's maker builds might be shared with other game makers. That is a build consequence (the format must be exportable), not a question now.
  • Which decision ahead depends on it. “How games and venues extend the engine”: what a pack may declare is in what it decides, and the difference between tables and rules is the difference between a configured engine and an interpreting one. “The published protocol's form”: the pack is one of the protocol's files, and a rule format is the largest thing it could carry. “Where authority lives across devices, offline and standalone included”: a rule runner on the tablet and on a hardware scanner alone is a second place rules are decided.

The options

  • Tables of values only.

    What it means Every new combination is an ability a developer adds, named in the pack's list of required abilities. The engine stays as today in kind; the hardware scanner's firmware stays simple.

    What it costs The designer's loop closes at every combination no declaration yet names, and a developer's additions carry all growth beyond the declared ones.

  • Declared when-then rules over the engine's abilities.

    What it means The pack names triggers the engine knows (a scan of this kind, a clock mark, a phase change, a cue finishing) and responses the engine knows (play, light, score, show, hold), and the designer wires them. Most new mechanics need no developer; the rule format is what adopters learn.

    What it costs A rule format inside the pack and so inside the published protocol; a check at publish that refuses rules the engine or the venue cannot run; a runner in the engine and, for standalone games, on the tablet; a listing on the GM's tablet; the hardware scanner either asks the engine (networked) or runs the subset a device alone can (standalone). It must be kept small or it becomes a programming language by accident.

  • Scripts in the pack.

    What it means The designer writes code into the pack and can do anything.
