Requirements

These are the engine's requirements, for you to confirm: the ten promises, what each person can count on at a given moment; the quality targets, how well the engine must work, each told as a situation that can be tested; the extension cases, how the platform must grow and who does the growing; and the standing rules from the roadmap. The panel and the design rounds design against them. They say what is needed, never how to build it.

Each promise reads the same way. Who and when name the people and the moment. What they count on is the promise in full; what already exists for it today is on Today's engine. The long-term goal is where the design is heading; a goal marked open is left to the design rounds. The quality targets and extension cases that serve the promise follow. Where today's engine falls short lists the design flaws that break it, each a situation on Today's engine. In a standalone game says what changes with no engine. Folded under the entry are what the planned capabilities need from it and the test situations taken from the shows. Each quality target gives what happens and what must then be true, the number left for you to set where one is needed, the promises it serves, whether it is settled or proposed, and why it matters. Each extension case names who extends, what they should need to touch, what must stay untouched, when, the promises it serves, its status and why.

Nothing on this page is a design. Where a target or a case rests on a decision you made or a roadmap section you ratified, it says so; where it is proposed, it waits for your word on Decisions, where every number left for you to set is listed in one place.

The designer's game is the game the whole kit plays

Who
The designer who publishes a game; the GM at doors-open; a second designer later
When
Between games when the designer publishes, and at doors-open when the GM checks that every device matches
What they count on

The whole kit plays that game, that version, with no code change: the engine scores by it, the TV shows its words, every tablet and scanner holds the same copy, and the GM can see at a glance that they match, or a mismatched device is refused. A second designer's game runs on the same engine.

The long-term goal

A designer builds a whole game as a pack and never touches code or settings files; the hardware scanner gets its rules as data, never new firmware. One list names every device and place that holds a copy of the pack; each copy shows its version, the Game record carries it, and a mismatch shows when a device connects. Media files are checked by a fingerprint of their content.

Where today's engine falls short Mainly
  • Pack edits reach each copy of the pack differently: The pack is read at scattered moments and no message says it changed, so the TV, the engine and a tablet can end up running different versions of one game.
Also
  • Names joining two places are kept by hand: The format of the files the pack hands each device is kept by hand on both sides, so the hardware scanner cannot read the pack's own token file.
  • The deployed game is many parts nothing compares: The videos and other media files the pack names are copied onto the show machine by hand, and nothing checks that every file the pack names is there.
  • Nothing declares a game standalone (proposed): Nothing in the pack says a game runs without an engine, yet it declares a scoreboard, a clock and videos that an engine-less device cannot show.

In a standalone game each device loads whatever pack copy it was given; a tablet reloading mid-game takes the newest pack; no record says which pack a game used. In the candidate shape the tablet runs Game rules and Pack channel: one pack per game, its version on the record.

What the planned capabilities need from it (6)
  • The pack and the check that refuses a broken pack: every part of the engine takes the game's tokens, rules, modes and cues from the pack, never from code; the engine refuses to start on a pack that fails the check and keeps the pack it loaded for the whole of a game; a new pack is taken up only between games, at a moment the lifecycle allows.
  • The second game's own modules, multi-tap player actions and moving the game's field names into the pack: scoring models, hidden facts, multi-tap actions and even the word for a score-holder come from the pack.
  • The pack's media files and clearing three old stopgaps from the code: the pack names its files by a fingerprint of their contents and carries no venue-hardware name.
  • Applying a change while the engine runs, between games, and the Pack channel: one complete list of every place that holds a copy of the pack. No document has this list today.
  • Tools for writing the game's content: a replacement for Notion feeds the same pack through the same check.
  • Hardware-scanner parity: a game mechanic that needs new firmware loaded onto every hardware scanner breaks the designer's edit-and-play loop, so everything a designer changes is pack data.

One game, the same on every screen

Who
The GM on any tablet; the audience reading the TV; in future games, a player with a hidden fact
When
Mid-show, at once, and after a tablet comes back
What they count on

Whatever the GM did on any tablet is what every tablet, the TV and the audience see: a delete frees the token everywhere, a reset empties every screen, and nobody has to ask which screen is right. In future games a player's hidden fact reaches only its owner.

The long-term goal

The Game record decides teams, claims, scores, evidence and phase: on the engine, or on the tablet in a standalone game, under the same rules. Delivery carries each fact by one route to the screens that may see it, live and after a reconnect; no screen keeps its own copy to decide from. Hidden facts never reach the wrong screen.

Where today's engine falls short Mainly
  • Each tablet keeps its own list of claimed tokens: Every tablet keeps its own list of claimed tokens that only grows, so after a delete every connected tablet keeps refusing the token the engine has freed, and a tablet that lost its connection during a Reset All Scores, and did not reload, keeps the old claims when it reconnects.
  • Reconnect snapshot and live update are built apart: What a tablet gets when it connects and what it gets live for the same fact come from different code, so a tablet that reloaded can differ from one that watched everything happen.
  • Tablets and phones decide facts the engine owns: Tablets and phones work out claims, points and 'seen before' from their own copy of the rules, so the result screen shows a verdict the engine has not given.
  • Several places answer the same question, each differently: One question has several answerers inside the engine or on one tablet, so 'is a video playing', 'which teams exist' and 'may I scan' get different answers in different places.
Also
  • Service health and service state travel on separate paths: Equipment state reaches the panels on a different route from its health, so a panel can look calm while the engine knows the player is down.
  • Nothing says what a game boundary clears or keeps: A reconnecting tablet guesses that a new game began from whatever arrives and may wipe its own history.
  • Boot and Reset System wire the engine differently: After a Reset System the engine reconnects the messages it sends every screen before the step that saves the game, so a later Reset All Scores sends every tablet and the TV the old transaction list, and cleared evidence comes back.
  • A reply never says which request, final or stored: A reply does not say which request it answers, so a tablet's copy and the engine's record stop matching.
  • Pack edits reach each copy of the pack differently: A tablet running a different pack than the engine is only written to a log.
  • Names joining two places are kept by hand: Team Details reads the score from a place the tablet never fills, so it shows the wrong score while the scoreboard screen shows the right one.

In a standalone game one tablet holds the only copy, so tablets cannot disagree, but not every screen refreshes; two tablets are two games, one per game undecided. In the candidate shape the tablet runs the engine's Game record and Delivery, or its own engine kept equal by tests.

What the planned capabilities need from it (6)
  • The check of every screen for false statements and the GM scanner redesign: a named decider for each fact the GM sees, applied to the ten kinds of service state the screens receive and the twenty-two kinds of message the GM tablet listens for; the redesign adds a new fact the GM writes, the game's phase.
  • Capturing the roster, notes and accusation, and capturing photos: each needs a decider, a place that survives a restart and a place in the reconnect catch-up. A note held only on a tablet is a copy, not a record.
  • The second game and players' phones: hidden facts have a decider and a saved home, and the engine filters what each screen receives.
  • The updates that carry each service's state: state and health travel together on one route, and every catch-up message carries every service. The same bug has come back four times.
  • A pass on every connection, and remote displays: the engine knows which screens are connected and which record each may see.
  • Applying a change while the engine runs, between games: every device is told that the pack changed.

Every tap gets its answer, and the night can be told afterwards

Who
The GM at a token or button and the player at a token; after the show, the director who tells the night's story, the players it is told to, and the owner and designer
When
During play at every tap, and after the game when the night is reviewed
What they count on

At a tap on a token or a button they learn at once whether it counted, why not if it did not, or that it is held for later or must be tried again; nothing is silently lost or doubled. Afterwards the director tells the players the story of their night from what was captured during play, with the game and the texts they saw, not reconstructed; how that story reaches the players is the game's own business.

The long-term goal

Every request gets one reply in the same form: which request it answers, the outcome, whether it is final, whether anything was stored. At the end, one record of the night is written from the facts decided and the roster, notes, accusation and photos captured during play. It carries the game it ran and the texts the players saw. The tablet's report and the game's own telling both read it; for ALN that telling is the Director Console, a separate program, and the record leaves room for it to run inside the engine's Review stage later. Whether refused scans are recorded stays open.

Who

When
In the days before a show, on a laptop or on the show machine between games
What they count on

The Design studio talks to the engine only by sending it requests, never by writing its files; the engine applies a change at a moment the Lifecycle allows, never mid-game, or refuses and says why. A separate trial copy of the engine runs as its own Engine instance, so it touches nothing live, and on a laptop the equipment reads as not installed. Where it runs is open.

The standing rules

The standing rules are the constraints the owner has ratified that hold for every block of work and every design, now and later. They are not promises or quality targets: they say what every block of work and every design must do or must never do, whatever shape the design takes. The panel and the design rounds must respect every one of them, and a design that breaks one is out.

They come from the roadmap's section on standing constraints, in its six groups: identity and player data; growth without corners; the media model; the design-iteration loop; platform parity; and the engine holds no opinion. The identity rules are also the privacy defaults that everyone who runs the open-source engine in future inherits. Each rule below says where it comes from and, where the roadmap gives one, the date the owner ratified it.
