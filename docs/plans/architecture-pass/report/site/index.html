What the engine promises

The engine runs shows for any game, not only ALN, and it is being redesigned for the long term: an engine that is intuitive and easy to maintain, to keep developing and to extend. This site holds the engine's requirements for you to confirm (what each person counts on, how well it must work, how it must grow, and the standing rules), the design decisions ahead and the few already made, today's engine as evidence, and everything decided, by title. What the engine is for

The engine is the game-independent software that runs a show, and it is going toward an open-source show platform that designers who are not technical can use and technical people can extend.

A game designer builds a whole game as a pack in the design tool, never touching code or configuration files, and tunes it by changing a value, applying it and playing.

A venue technician fills in a profile of what the venue has, connects the pack's names to the real instruments in the room on one page, and runs one preflight that answers go or no-go, with every line showing where its answer comes from.

A GM runs the show from four sections (Game Ops, Show Control, Environment, Game Admin), and the post-game report builds itself from what was captured during play.

A player taps tokens on a phone or a shared station with no account, no login, no app to install and no recorded identity unless the game designs an identification moment.

The business is one production, one kit and many games on it; a second kit or operator is not planned, but must stay cheap to start, so nothing in the engine may assume one kit, one pack or one production.

The standing rules: players' data stays on the kit; media never lives inside the pack; phones and hardware scanners stay equal; the engine holds no opinion about game design; red always means act now; the preflight states its own limits; and nothing deploys to the live machine until the owner says the new engine is ready.

A second, toy game stays with the engine's code for good, so every change is proven on two games and ALN stays one pack among others.

Who counts on the engine, and when

Author and Rehearse

In the days before a show, on a laptop or on the show machine between games, when a designer builds a game, changes a value, publishes and plays a round.

Who is there
  • The game designer
  • The owner, designing the second game
  • A new designer, learning from the toy game
  • The designer reading a badge on a draft

  • Anyone reading a screen can act on it without a second questionThe badge on a draft uses the same go and no-go words as the show, and a desk check never passes for a room check.
  • Each person sees their part and can do only their partDesigners reach their tools and only those.

Before doors

In the days before an event and at doors-open, when the venue technician fits the game to the venue and the preflight answers go or no-go.

Who is there
  • The venue technician
  • The GM at doors-open
  • A future operator with a second kit
  • A stranger on the venue wifi
What they count on
  • The game fits the venue it is in tonightThe venue technician states what the kit brings tonight, binds the pack's names to real instruments and gets one go or no-go; a second kit or a new machine needs no change to engine code.

During play

Mid-show, at every tap on a token or a button, every command and every glance at a screen; also on a night when the game runs with no engine by choice.

Who is there
  • The GM running the live show: at a token, a button, the start and pause buttons, or the Environment and Show Control sections
  • The players, each at a token, with no account
  • The audience, reading the TV and watching the clock
  • In future games, a player with a hidden fact
  • A GM running a game with no engine
  • A stranger on the venue wifi
What they count on
  • Every tap gets its answer, and the night can be told afterwardsAt a tap on a token or a button, the GM or the player learns at once whether it counted, why not if it did not, or that it is held for later or must be tried again; nothing is silently lost or doubled.
  • One game, the same on every screenWhatever the GM did on any tablet is what every tablet, the TV and the audience see, also after a tablet comes back; in future games a player's hidden fact reaches only its owner.
  • The room does what the designer cued and the GM askedThe room does what the designer cued and what the GM asked; the GM sees it happen, or sees at once that it did not and what to press; a dead program comes back by itself before anyone notices.
  • Each lifecycle button does exactly what its name saysA pause blocks scans, and a restart during a show loses nothing.
  • Each person sees their part and can do only their partThe GM alone can start, pause, end and change scores, and the engine knows which tablet did it; the audience sees scores and exposed evidence and nothing else; a player sees the memory they scanned; a stranger sees nothing.
  • Anyone reading a screen can act on it without a second questionRed means act now, grey means absent on purpose, old looks old, and the TV's clock never counts on its own.
  • The game fits the venue it is in tonightA game declared standalone knows it is standalone instead of showing a lost connection all night.
  • A change is proven before it reaches anyone, and the owner alone sends it to the showNo test or rehearsal ever reaches a live show.

Release day

Before every change reaches anyone, and on the day the owner sends a new version to the venue.

Who is there
  • The developer changing the engine
  • The owner sending a version to the venue
What they count on

How well it must work

  • Staying up through a live showA power cut, a crashed music player, a tablet's wifi dropping or a batch of scans sent twice must leave the game the same game: no scan lost or doubled, every tablet back by itself under its own station name, and a held cue shown as held.
  • Every screen telling the truthNo screen shows a calm, current value for a fact it cannot confirm: a silent engine reads as offline within a set time, old numbers look old, grey means not installed tonight, red appears only when something that should work does not, every preflight line says paper or live, and a tablet never judges a scan before the engine does.
  • The designer's iteration speedA designer changes a value and plays it within a set number of minutes with no restart, a broken pack is refused at the desk with the fault named, a trial copy of the engine runs beside the show without touching it, and a whole game is built in the design tool with no code or settings file.
  • Operating it without technical helpA venue technician stands the kit up from the guide and gets one go or no-go with no terminal; the machine powers on straight into a running show; a dropped speaker gives the GM a red row with the actions on it; and the engine writes the record of the night by itself, with one clock.
  • Easy to understand and change, for people and for AI agentsEvery fact a screen shows is decided in exactly one place; everything that crosses the engine's edge is written once in the published protocol; a newcomer, person or agent, finds the glossary, the promises and the protocol in one sitting and proves a small change within a set time; and no word of ALN's lives in the engine.
  • Easy to extendA second game with new scoring, hidden facts and multi-tap actions runs on the pack, and a capability the engine lacks is added without touching the games already running; a new kind of device joins by its pass; a swapped lighting system or video player changes only the code that talks to it and the venue profile; and a second production's kit needs a new profile and no change to engine code.
  • Testable without the live showA whole game runs end to end on a laptop with stand-ins for video, sound, lights and speakers, the same way at every test level; every change is tested on ALN's pack and the toy game's; a skipped test fails loudly; every stand-in is compared with the real thing; and a test run on the show machine cannot touch the live show.
  • Privacy and accessPlayers have no account, and the engine records a device or session id and nothing about the person unless the game designs an identification moment; game data stays on the kit and is kept only as long as you set, per game; a stranger on the venue wifi gets nothing; only staff who log in can start, pause, end or reset a game; and the engine filters what each screen receives before it sends it.
  • Running well on the show machine and the venue network at the size of a showOne show machine, a few tablets, scanners and screens on the kit's own network with no internet run a full two-hour game with headroom, within set times and limits; the kit's capacity for phones is measured before the first phone is admitted; and a pack stays small and names its media by fingerprint.
  • Standalone and offlineA game declared standalone says standalone, never disconnected, and the tablet decides under the same rules as the engine, proven equal; no game mixes devices on an engine with devices working alone; a standalone game's record names the pack it ran; and a scanner that loses the network keeps its scans and sends them once.
  • Safe release and rollbackThe show machine carries one written list of what it should hold and a check names any piece that differs; nothing reaches the machine until you say the new engine is ready; and replugging the old machine brings everything back.
  • More than one copy, kit or pack without reworkTwo copies of the engine share one machine without touching each other's files, ports, programs or passes; a kit holds a library of installed packs and the operator picks one per event; and a second kit needs a profile and nothing else.

How it must grow

Designers

A designer extends the platform through the pack and its media bundle, written in the design tool and published through the engine's check: a whole second game (A whole second game, as a pack), one value changed and played between shows (Change one value between shows and play it), a new way to turn in a token (A third way to turn in a token), new tokens with their media (Adding tokens and their media files), a cue that names a lighting role (A cue names roles, the venue binds them), the game's own words and look (Each game's own words, colours and labels), acts (A game divided into acts), what a scan causes (The pack says what a scan causes), who is named and what each device may do (Who may do what on which device), a game declared standalone (A small game with no show machine; where the declaration lives, with the pack, the venue profile or the game's record, is your choice) and a rehearsal on a trial copy of the engine, with a profile generated from the pack and bound to stand-ins (Rehearsing the whole show on stand-ins). They never touch engine code, settings files or firmware.

Developers

A developer extends it once, as platform work, at the places the engine leaves for additions, and other games run as before: a scoring model (A new scoring model added once), a new kind of screen (Configuring screens, and a new kind of screen), a new device kind (Players' own phones and two-tap actions), a new program the engine drives (A different lighting system or video player), a content source (A different content tool feeds the same pack) and the second game's modules (The owner's second game needs new modules).

Developers from outside the team

A stranger extends it through the published protocol, the toy game as a worked example and the guides, and sends the change back with the developer's tests, which show the show still runs for both games; they never see our secrets, our pack or our venue (A stranger extends the platform from the guide).

Venue technicians

A venue technician fits the game to a new room through the venue profile alone: what the room has tonight, each of the pack's names bound to a real instrument on one page, and one preflight that says go or no-go (A new venue is a new profile); equipment left at home reads as not installed tonight.

Operators: you, or a future one

An operator extends it through their own profiles, installed packs and per-kit settings: a second production's kit (A second production takes the platform), a new engine version swapped in by replugging (A new engine version by swapping machines), a different installed game per event (A different installed game each night) and how long records are kept (The owner sets how long records are kept).

Directors

A director, with the game's own developer, extends the after-game telling through the night's record, a published file read from outside the engine; what reaches the players afterwards stays the game's business (The director tells the night from the record).

The words

The site's own words come first, then the names of the steps of the work, then the words the design rounds use; each situation on Today's engine ends with the reason restated in them.

This site's words

Promise
What a person can count on at a given moment. Parts keep promises; design flaws break them. The ten promises are the first of the requirements.
Moment
When a promise matters: a production stage (Author, Rehearse, Run, Review), a point in a show, or release day.
Quality
Something every part must have so a promise holds everywhere, like every screen telling the truth, or testable without the live show. It keeps promises; it is never a promise or a part. The twelve qualities on this page group the quality targets.
Quality target
One situation under a quality, told so it can be tested: what happens, then what must be true. It says what is needed, never how to build it; where a number is needed and no source gives one, you set it.
Extension case
One way the platform must be able to grow, told as a situation: who does the growing, what they should need to touch, and what must stay untouched.
The standing rules
The roadmap's rules that bind every block: players never have accounts; the engine's only built-in identity is the device or session id, and naming a person is game policy, never an engine default; game data stays on the kit, and how long it is kept is your decision, per game; media never lives inside the pack; a game is playtested with no code or settings file, by changing a value in the tool, applying it and playing it; phones and hardware scanners stay equal in what the engine lets them do, not in their screens; the engine holds no opinion about game design; nothing assumes one kit, one pack or one production.
Design flaw
Something in today's design that breaks a promise. The sixteen are test situations any new design must handle, never the reason for a design.
The published protocol
One versioned definition of everything that crosses the engine's edge: what a device may ask, what the engine answers, what it tells screens, and the files that carry a game or a night (the pack, the venue profile, the night's record, the media list). Every program's code is generated from it or checked against it. It is designed in an early design round, not merely collected.
The pack
A whole game as data: its tokens and their content, modes, scoring, groups, cues, the clock, its words and its look, written in the design tool and published through the engine's check that refuses a broken pack. Media files are not inside it; the pack names them by a fingerprint of their content.
The venue profile
What the room has tonight, filled in by the venue technician: which speakers, which lights, which network, how many stations and screens, and which real instrument each of the pack's names binds to. A second venue is a second profile; the pack is the same.
The night's record
The one record the engine writes when a game ends: the facts it decided, with the roster, the notes, the accusation and the photos captured during play, the pack and version the game ran, and the texts the players saw. The director tells the night from it.
Media list and media bundle
The pack's list of its media files, each named by a fingerprint of its content, and the bundle that carries the files themselves and moves between machines; a missing or changed file is named before the show.
The earlier candidate
The 17-part shape of the engine drawn bottom-up from today's engine, on this site since version 5. It is one input among the panel's: the panel compares it with its own proposals in its second round. Nothing in it is decided.
Part
One of the 17 pieces of the earlier candidate. It runs on the engine or on a device.
Architecture pass questions
The nine questions the architecture pass asks of the whole engine: who decides each fact the GM sees; whether a service's state travels with its health; how the engine reads the programs it runs; whether anything but the live engine can touch the live show; whether one question gets one answer; what each fact does between games (a start, an end, a reset, a restart, a pack change); whether each screen gets only what its role needs; how copies update and whether a screen shows their age; and whether the catch-up after a reconnect matches the live message. Since the design rounds go by the hardest decisions to reverse, the nine serve as checks on each design; that reading is to be confirmed with you. They are not the questions on Decisions, which wait for your answer.
Venue technician
The person who fits a kit to a venue: fills in the venue profile, binds the pack's names to the real instruments in the room, and runs the preflight.
Director
The person who tells the night's story at the Review stage and carries what was learned back into Author; usually the same person as the GM or the designer.
Paper or live
Checked from the files only, or checked against the equipment in the room.
A device's pass
What a device shows when it connects: who it is and what it may do.
Door
A way into or out of the engine that requests or facts pass through. Today's code has several. The earlier candidate had two: Answering, the one door every request comes in by, and Delivery, the one door every fact goes out by; the published protocol now defines what crosses the engine's edge. It is not the reason a service is not installed tonight, which the code also calls a door; see Not installed tonight.
Not installed tonight
Grey, absent on purpose, never a fault: tonight's profile leaves the service out. The code calls this state dormant. A service the GM takes out of service during the show is dormant too, for its own reason, and reads "Out of service", also grey.
Health words
Healthy, down and not installed tonight (grey): the three words every service's health is reported in.
Label
What every fact and every reply carries to a screen: how old it is and how sure the engine is of it, so a screen can show "old" or "unknown" instead of a calm normal value.
Blue, green
Blue is the show machine in use now, left untouched by this work; the game runs on it until the new engine is ready. Green is the new machine this work builds. Swapping in green is done by replugging, and replugging swaps back. A "green" test run, as in the title of A green test run proves nothing about the show, is a passing one, unrelated.
The toy game
A small second game kept beside ALN for good, so the engine is always tested on a game that is not ALN. It also shows a new designer how a game is made.
Planned capability
One thing the engine is planned to be able to do, such as restarting a crashed program by itself. The roadmap you approved lists 52; each promise shows the ones that need something from it.
The test bench
The developer's tests, kept outside the engine, where a thin first version of the new design runs end to end at the start of the build half. The tests run the same engine, the same pack and the same checks at every test level.
The test rig
An automated machine that runs the real engine with real video, music, audio and lighting stand-ins, so a design flaw can be made to happen for real instead of being read off the code. It was lost when the cloud computer it ran on was wiped; it is built again when the build half starts, as part of the test bench, not before design.
Pi 4
The small computer that shows the scoreboard on a second screen.

The names of the steps

The architecture pass
The current work: the redesign of the whole engine for the long term, in two halves. The game keeps running on blue until the new engine is ready to swap in on green, and no date governs the work.
The design half, the build half
The pass's two halves. The design half, now, decides the shared design with you, one question round at a time, and ends with the new roadmap. The build half changes the engine to follow the design, one area at a time, starting with a thin first version on the new test bench.
The requirements
The design half's step now: the promises with your answers, the quality targets as testable situations, the extension cases with who does the extending, and the standing rules. You confirm them on Decisions.
The panel
Three independent architects with different lenses who have not seen the orchestrator's reasoning. They propose the top-level design blind after you confirm the requirements, then critique each other and the earlier candidate. Its brief comes to you before it runs.
The design rounds
After the panel: one round per hard-to-reverse decision, the hardest first, each with two or three designs side by side, checked against the promises, the quality targets, the extension cases and the sixteen design flaws. Each round comes to you as its own page.
The build plan
After the design rounds: the order of the build half's areas, starting with a thin first version of the new design running end to end.
The hostile review
Two reviewers with different angles and a checker for each finding, one group of reviewers at a time; a finding that would change a decision comes back to you as a question.
The final questions
The last question round before sign-off. Every decision is made in a question round with the evidence in view, never at sign-off.
The owner's sign-off
A skim with no new decisions. It ends the design half and makes the new roadmap the roadmap's next revision.
Question rounds
The rounds in which you answer, named by date: the definition session of 2026-10-30, the round of 2026-10-30 to 2026-11-01, and the after-game survey of 2026-11-01.
The blocks
The roadmap's units of work, by name: the unlock block, the hardening block, the truth sweep (its charter retired), the capture block, the preview block and the depth-and-close block; then the later blocks: players' phones, the second game's modules, content tooling, the operations era and the release block.
The readiness states
Coherent on main (reached), hardware-proven, show-ready, previewable and adoptable. Deployment and sharing decisions are made at these states.
Test levels
By name: logic only; real programs with stand-in hardware; the home test bed; the venue. The same engine, pack and checks run at every level.

The words the design rounds use

Module
Any piece of code with an inside and an outside, from one function to the whole engine.
Interface
Everything other code must know to use a module.
Seam
Where one module meets another, and where behaviour can be swapped.
Deep, shallow
A deep module does a lot behind a small interface. A shallow one makes its callers know almost as much as it does.
Locality
A fix in one place fixes it everywhere.
Leverage
One module serving many callers.
Adapter
The real thing plugged in at a seam, like VLC behind "play a video".
