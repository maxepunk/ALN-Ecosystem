What the engine is for

The engine is the game-independent software that runs a show, and it is going toward an open-source show platform that designers who are not technical can use and technical people can extend.

A game designer builds a whole game as a pack in the design tool, never touching code or configuration files, and tunes it by changing a value, applying it and playing.

A venue technician fills in a profile of what the venue has, connects the pack's names to the real instruments in the room on one page, and runs one preflight that answers go or no-go with every line traceable.

A GM runs the show from four sections (Game Ops, Show Control, Environment, Game Admin), and the post-game report builds itself from what was captured during play.

A player taps tokens on a phone or a shared station with no account, no login, no app to install and no recorded identity unless the game designs an identification moment.

The business is one production, one kit and many games on it; a second kit or operator is not planned, but must stay cheap to start, so nothing in the engine may assume one kit, one pack or one production.

The standing rules: players' data stays on the kit; media never lives inside the pack; phones and hardware scanners stay equal; the engine holds no opinion about game design; red always means act now; the preflight states its own limits; and nothing deploys to the live machine until the owner says the new engine is ready.

A second, toy game stays in the repository for good, so every change is proven on two games and ALN stays one pack among others.

Who counts on the engine, and when

Author and Rehearse

In the days before a show, on a laptop or on the show machine between games, when a designer builds a game, changes a value, publishes and plays a round.

Who is there
  • The game designer
  • The owner, designing the second game
  • A new designer, learning from the toy game
  • The designer reading a badge on a draft
What they count on
  • The designer changes a value and plays it within minutes, and the live show never noticesBuild a game or change one value in it, publish and apply it, and play a round within minutes; the engine's own check refuses a bad pack at the desk; nothing done here can reach the live show or its records.
  • Anyone reading a screen can act on it without a second questionThe badge on a draft uses the same go and no-go words as the show, and a desk check never passes for a room check.
  • Each person sees their part and can do only their partDesigners reach their tools and only those.

Before doors

In the days before an event and at doors-open, when the venue technician fits the game to the venue and the preflight answers go or no-go.

Who is there
  • The venue technician
  • The GM at doors-open
  • A future operator with a second kit
  • A stranger on the venue wifi
What they count on
  • The game fits the venue it is in tonightThe venue technician states what the kit brings tonight, binds the pack's names to real instruments and gets one go or no-go; a second kit or a new machine needs no change to engine code.
  • Anyone reading a screen can act on it without a second questionThe venue technician's go is a go: every line says paper or live, and a desk check never passes for a room check.
  • The designer's game is the game the whole kit playsEvery tablet and scanner holds the same copy of the pack, and the GM sees at a glance that they match, or a mismatched device is refused.
  • Each person sees their part and can do only their partVenue technicians reach their tools and only those; a stranger on the venue wifi sees nothing.

During play

Mid-show, at every tap on a token or a button, every command and every glance at a screen; also on a night when the game runs with no engine by choice.

Who is there
  • The GM running the live show: at a token, a button, the start and pause buttons, or the Environment and Show Control sections
  • The players, each at a token, with no account
  • The audience, reading the TV and watching the clock
  • In future games, a player with a hidden fact
  • A GM running a game with no engine
  • A stranger on the venue wifi
What they count on
  • Every tap gets its answer, and the night can be told afterwardsAt a tap on a token or a button, the GM or the player learns at once whether it counted, why not if it did not, or that it is held for later or must be tried again; nothing is silently lost or doubled.
  • One game, the same on every screenWhatever the GM did on any tablet is what every tablet, the TV and the audience see, also after a tablet comes back; in future games a player's hidden fact reaches only its owner.
  • The room does what the designer cued and the GM askedThe room does what the designer cued and what the GM asked; the GM sees it happen, or sees at once that it did not and what to press; a dead program comes back by itself before anyone notices.
  • Each lifecycle button does exactly what its name saysA pause blocks scans, and a restart during a show loses nothing.
  • Each person sees their part and can do only their partThe GM alone can start, pause, end and change scores, and the engine knows which tablet did it; the audience sees scores and exposed evidence and nothing else; a player sees the memory they scanned; a stranger sees nothing.
  • Anyone reading a screen can act on it without a second questionRed means act now, grey means absent on purpose, old looks old, and the TV's clock never counts on its own.
  • The game fits the venue it is in tonightA game declared standalone knows it is standalone instead of showing a lost connection all night.
  • A change is proven before it reaches anyone, and the owner alone sends it to the showNo test or rehearsal ever reaches a live show.

The end of a game, and between games

When the GM ends or resets a game and before the next one starts; the designer's publish lands here, and a pack change is taken up only here.

Who is there
  • The GM who ends and resets games
  • The designer who publishes a game, or a second designer later
What they count on
  • Each lifecycle button does exactly what its name saysAn ended game stays ended and no scan lands in it; the next game starts clean; a between-games edit never reaches the game in progress.
  • The designer's game is the game the whole kit playsThe whole kit plays the game the designer published, that version, with no code change; a second designer's game runs on the same engine.
  • The designer changes a value and plays it within minutes, and the live show never noticesThe designer publishes and applies a change between games, and the live show never notices.

Review

After the game, when the night is reviewed and told.

Who is there
  • The director who tells the night's story
  • The players it is told to
  • The owner and the designer
What they count on
  • Every tap gets its answer, and the night can be told afterwardsThe director tells the players the story of their night from what was captured during play, with the game and the texts they saw, not reconstructed; how that story reaches the players is the game's own business.

Release day

Before every change reaches anyone, and on the day the owner sends a new version to the venue.

Who is there
  • The developer changing the engine
  • The owner sending a version to the venue
What they count on
  • A change is proven before it reaches anyone, and the owner alone sends it to the showWhen every test passes, the show still runs for ALN and for the toy game; the owner decides when a new version goes to the venue, the machine carries one matching set, and the owner can swap back by replugging a machine.

Decision 22 adds a requirement every rule must serve: a whole game can run with no engine, the GM tablet and the player scanners each working alone, as a choice the game declares, and no game mixes networked and standalone devices. Each promise and each card says what changes with no engine; the standalone pass, with the hardware scanner's parity table, is on Design flaws.

How it fits together

What happens at every tap. In the candidate shape, four promises meet at every tap in a networked game. The designer's pack sets the rules, which Game rules alone calculates (The designer's game is the game the whole kit plays). The Game record, the same on every screen, is what the tap is judged against (One game, the same on every screen). The request comes in through Answering, the one door in, and the answer goes back to the GM or the player; an accepted tap joins the record (Every tap gets its answer, and the night can be told afterwards). Connections and roles say who tapped and who may see the result (Each person sees their part and can do only their part). Delivery sends every fact that changed to every screen that may see it.

The engine's own machinery. In the candidate, Equipment reports what each program in the room is doing, dated. Verdict turns that, and the venue technician's profile in Venue fit, into go, no-go or "not installed tonight" (The game fits the venue it is in tonight, Anyone reading a screen can act on it without a second question). Show runner holds the cues, the clock, the video queue and the items held while a service is down, so the room does what was cued and asked (The room does what the designer cued and the GM asked). Lifecycle says when to check again and what each part resets at setup, start, pause, end, reset, restart and the next game, which feeds back into the equipment (Each lifecycle button does exactly what its name says); it alone moves a game. The profile feeds this loop from outside. In today's code the loop is also a chain of seven backend modules that depend on each other in a circle, and the game's record is one of them.

What reaches the show from outside. In the candidate, the designer's week and the developer's proof reach a live show only through the pack and through each engine having its own names. Design studio talks to the engine by message, and its publish goes out through Pack channel, one list of every holder of the pack with a version stamp on every copy (The designer changes a value and plays it within minutes, and the live show never notices). Engine instance gives each engine its own data folder, ports, process files and passes, so a test, a preview or a second kit never touches the live one; Joins and proofs run on an engine of their own and on two packs, and Release compares what a machine carries against one described set (A change is proven before it reaches anyone, and the owner alone sends it to the show).

The night's record, after the end. In the candidate, when the GM ends a game, Lifecycle closes it and The night's record writes one record of the night from the facts the engine decided, with the roster, notes, accusation and photos captured into it during play. It carries the game it ran and the texts the players saw. The director tells the players the story of their night from it, and the owner and the designer read it too. This is the second half of the tap promise: the night can be told afterwards. The designer's Review stage reads it inside the engine; for ALN the Director Console reads it from outside (decision 28). Today nothing writes this record; the tablet builds a report from its own copies.

Around the engine

Notion

The database where ALN's memories are written: each token's text, its rating, its type and its group.

How it connectsThe sync script reads it and writes the pack files. The engine never reads Notion; it takes the game only from the pack. ALN's Director Console still reads memory texts here, not from the pack the game ran.

StatusToday's source for ALN's content. Paper documents are not in the pack at all; whether they join it, with "unlocked" recorded during play, is a question for step 3 (decision 28).

The sync script

A Python script that turns the Notion database into the pack's token file, derives the group multipliers from Notion's authoring shorthand, draws the display images and regenerates the pack manifest.

How it connectsIts output is the pack files folder; in the candidate it is one source of the pack that Pack channel adopts, and nothing else in the engine knows it exists.

StatusToday it is one of the nine holders of the pack that nothing joins (C12).

The pack files

The shared folder of token definitions, the rules file (scoring, modes, groups, the duplicate policy, the game clock) and a manifest with a fingerprint of the whole.

How it connectsThe engine freezes a snapshot of it at boot; the GM tablet and the phone carry nested copies; the hardware scanner downloads it from the engine. In the candidate Pack channel is the one list of holders, one notice that the pack changed, and a version stamp every copy shows and every game record carries; Game rules takes its rules only from here.

StatusToday the pack is held in nine places and no message says it changed, so after a publish the TV, the engine and a tablet can run three versions of one game (Pack edits reach each copy of the pack differently, Names joining two places are kept by hand).

The design studio, today the config tool

The designer's drafts and their publishing, and the venue technician's profile editor, in a web tool beside the engine.

How it connectsToday it writes the engine's settings, routing file and media folders, imports engine modules by path and runs the engine's pack check as a child process; an edit shows at the engine's next restart. In the candidate Design studio talks to the engine only by message, applies at a window the lifecycle allows, and rehearses on a preview engine kept apart by Engine instance.

StatusToday two design flaws touch it: pack edits reach each copy differently, and the engine and the tests both assume they own the machine. Whether it stays a separate program or becomes a surface the engine itself serves is a choice for step 3.

Video and the TV

VLC, which plays the show's videos on the TV, and the TV's browser, which shows the scoreboard page.

How it connectsIn the candidate, both sit behind Equipment as adapters; Show runner queues videos and holds one while the player is busy or down. The browser is also a client of the engine under a watch-only pass, and the engine launches, shows, hides and kills it as an outside program.

StatusToday the engine's picture of VLC carries no age and is re-read only in part (Engine trusts its stale picture of outside programs); the scoreboard page decides which modes count as evidence from a file it fetched (Pack edits reach each copy of the pack differently). One interface per capability or per program is a choice for step 3.

Music

MPD, the music player, with its playlists.

How it connectsIn the candidate, an adapter behind Equipment; the show runner ducks it under a video or a sound.

StatusToday it keeps a private connected flag beside the shared health list, and its panel can keep a calm playing look while the engine knows the player is down (Service health and service state travel on separate paths).

Audio and Bluetooth

PipeWire, which routes video, music and sounds to the HDMI or the Bluetooth sink and sets each volume; the sound player; and BlueZ, which pairs and connects the Bluetooth speaker.

How it connectsIn the candidate, adapters behind Equipment; the venue technician's profile in Venue fit says which sink is which.

StatusToday these are three of the engine's seven adapters, with no shared interface above them and two supervision shapes (C3); the tests adopt the live audio and Bluetooth (C8).

Lighting

Home Assistant, which holds the scenes the designer's cues name.

How it connectsIn the candidate, an adapter behind Equipment; a scene name in the pack binds to a real scene through Venue fit.

StatusToday the lighting panel stays unavailable until a reload if Home Assistant was down at engine start (Service health and service state travel on separate paths). On the test rig a witness light per lighting role stands in for the room.

The after-game article: ALN's Director Console

A web app on the director's Windows computer, outside the kit. An AI writing pipeline waits at eleven stops for the director, who marks which paper documents the players unlocked, gives the roster with pronouns, pastes the accusation, the GM tablet's report and notes, picks and edits the story, names who is in each photo and approves the article, published as a web page two to eleven days after the game.

How it connectsToday it parses the tablet's downloaded markdown report with an AI call, reads memory texts and paper documents from Notion rather than the pack the game ran, takes the roster, notes and photos by hand, and shifts money events stamped by two clocks back into line (Tablets and phones decide facts the engine owns). In the candidate it reads The night's record, which carries the pack the game ran and the texts the players saw, so the article quotes what they held.

StatusDecided (28): plan for the console staying a separate program that reads the engine's record, and design the record so the console can later become ALN's own part of the engine's Review stage.

The emailer

An interactive script on the same computer. The director pastes the booking list, an optional note and a photo folder; it sends each player a thank-you email from the game's Gmail account with the article link, the feedback form link for that date, a return-visit discount code and the photos, and keeps a list of who was sent what on that computer.

How it connectsOutside the engine. It reads the article's address and the booking list, and nothing from the engine or the night's record.

StatusDecided (28): what the players receive after the show is the game's business, and the engine makes them no promise of their own.

The booking list and the ticketing service

Each player's name and email, from the bookings for a date.

How it connectsPasted into the emailer by the director today. It never enters the engine: a player taps tokens with no account, no login and no recorded identity unless the game designs an identification moment.

StatusDecided (28): players' emails stay outside the engine. Later the game's own tools could collect them from the ticketing service's API.

The words

The site's own words come first. The rest are the words step 3 uses; each card on Design flaws ends with the reason restated in them.

This site's words

Promise
What a person can count on at a given moment. Parts keep promises; design flaws break them.
Moment
When a promise matters: a production stage (Author, Rehearse, Run, Review), a point in a show, or release day.
Design flaw
Something in today's design that breaks a promise.
Part
One of the 17 pieces of the candidate design. It runs on the engine or on a device.
Quality
Something every part must have so a promise holds everywhere, like honest or proven. It keeps promises; it is never a promise or a part.
Architecture pass questions
The nine questions the architecture pass asks of the whole engine, numbered 1 to 9, such as who decides each fact the GM sees (1) and whether one question gets one answer (5). Each is answered by a rule. They are not the questions on Decisions, which wait for your answer.
Director
The person who tells the night's story at the Review stage and carries what was learned back into Author; usually the same person as the GM or the designer.
Paper or live
Checked from the files only, or checked against the equipment in the room.
A device's pass
What a device shows when it connects: who it is and what it may do.
Door
A way into or out of the engine that requests or facts pass through. Today's code has several. The candidate has two: Answering, the one door every request comes in by, and Delivery, the one door every fact goes out by. It is not the reason a service is dormant, which the code also calls a door; see Not installed tonight.
Not installed tonight
Grey, absent on purpose, never a fault: tonight's profile leaves the service out. The code calls this state dormant. A service the GM takes out of service during the show is dormant too, for its own reason, and reads "Out of service", also grey.

The words step 3 uses

Module
Any piece of code with an inside and an outside, from one function to the whole backend.
Interface
Everything other code must know to use a module.
Seam
Where one module meets another, and where behaviour can be swapped.
Deep, shallow
A deep module does a lot behind a small interface. A shallow one makes its callers know almost as much as it does.
Locality
A fix in one place fixes it everywhere.
Leverage
One module serving many callers.
Adapter
The real thing plugged in at a seam, like VLC behind "play a video".
