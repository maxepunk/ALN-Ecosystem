Promises

A promise is what a person can count on at a given moment. These ten are the engine's requirements, each said from the side of the people who count on it: who, when, and what must happen for them. The table at the top gives all ten at a glance with the parts that would keep each and the design flaws that break it; the two diagrams under it show how the promises depend on each other, the second folded. Whether these are the right ten, and whether three of them should split, is the promises question and A to G on Decisions.

Each entry reads the same way. The title is the promise. Who and when name the people and the moment. What they count on is the promise in full. Built today says what already exists for it. The long-term goal is where the design is heading; where it is marked open, step 3 settles it. Kept by names the parts of the candidate shape that would keep it, each a link to Parts. Broken by lists the design flaws that break it today, the main one first, each with one line on how; the cards are on Design flaws. In a standalone game says what changes with no engine. Folded under the entry are what the planned capabilities need from it and the test cases taken from the shows.

The promises wait for your confirmation; the parts named under each are a candidate. Each promise's earlier wording, and what the relationship map changed, are on Change log.

The ten at a glance

How each promise is kept. Most promises are kept by one or two parts of their own. Two are kept by a quality every part must have, together with a few parts of their own: "Anyone reading a screen can act on it without a second question" by a label on every fact (how sure, how old), and "A change is proven before it reaches anyone, and the owner alone sends it to the show" by tests every part passes. "Each lifecycle button does exactly what its name says" rests on a table of moments (setup, start, pause, end, reset, restart, the next game) that most other promises read.

How the promises depend on each other

The ten promises

The designer's game is the game the whole kit plays

Who
The designer who publishes a game; the GM at doors-open; a second designer later
When
Between games when the designer publishes, and at doors-open when the GM checks that every device matches
What they count on

The whole kit plays that game, that version, with no code change: the engine scores by it, the TV shows its words, every tablet and scanner holds the same copy, and the GM can see at a glance that they match, or a mismatched device is refused. A second designer's game runs on the same engine.

Built today

Packs that hold tokens, modes, scoring, groups and cues; a gate that refuses a broken pack; a second, toy game kept beside ALN.

The long-term goal

A designer builds a whole game as a pack and never touches code or settings files; the hardware scanner gets its rules as data, never new firmware. One list names every holder; each copy shows its version, the Game record carries it, and a mismatch shows when a device connects. Media files are checked by a fingerprint of their content.

Kept by these parts of the candidate shapeGame rulesPack channel
Broken by these design flaws today, the main one first
  • C12The pack is read at scattered moments and no message says it changed, so the TV, the engine and a tablet can end up running different versions of one game.
  • C14The shape of the files the pack hands each device is kept by hand on both sides, so the hardware scanner cannot read the pack's own token file.
  • C15The videos and other media files the pack names are copied onto the show machine by hand, and nothing checks that every file the pack names is there.
  • N1The pack has no field that says a game runs without an engine, yet it declares a scoreboard, a clock and videos that an engine-less device cannot show. (proposed)

In a standalone game each device loads whatever pack copy it was given; a tablet reloading mid-game takes the newest pack; no record says which pack a game used. In the candidate shape the tablet runs Game rules and Pack channel: one pack per game, its version on the record.

What the planned capabilities need from it (6)
  • The pack (capability 1) and the activation gate (capability 2): every part of the engine takes the game's tokens, rules, modes and cues from the pack, never from code; the engine refuses to start on a pack that fails the check and keeps the pack it adopted for the whole of a game; a new pack is taken up only between games, at a moment the lifecycle allows.
  • The second game's modules (capability 38), interaction primitives (capability 36) and the entity-field migration (capability 37): scoring models, hidden facts, multi-tap actions and even the word for a score-holder come from the pack.
  • The media bundle (capability 40) and the ledger retirements (capability 50): the pack names its files by a fingerprint of their contents and carries no venue-hardware name.
  • Hot-apply (capability 16) and the pack channel (capability 3): one complete list of every place that holds a copy of the pack. No document has this list today.
  • Content tooling (capability 41): a replacement for Notion feeds the same pack through the same gate.
  • Hardware-scanner parity (capability 49): a mechanic that needs a fleet re-flash breaks the designer's loop, so everything a designer changes is pack data.
Test cases from the shows (4); Answers the pass's questions 6, 5, 8
  1. Edit one token's rating on disk with the engine running and no restart: the token list, the pack file and the points a scan awards all agree, and the file's fingerprint matches the one in the engine's own list of pack files.
  2. Connect a tablet that runs a different pack: the GM sees a mismatch on screen, or the tablet is refused.
  3. Run the group-completion test on the real pack and on the toy pack: it runs on both and does not skip.
  4. With no alternate pack chosen, compare the phone scanner's token file with the engine's own pack: they are the same version.

One game, the same on every screen

Who
The GM on any tablet; the audience reading the TV; in future games, a player with a hidden fact
When
Mid-show, at once, and after a tablet comes back
What they count on

Whatever the GM did on any tablet is what every tablet, the TV and the audience see: a delete frees the token everywhere, a reset empties every screen, and nobody has to ask which screen is right. In future games a player's hidden fact reaches only its owner.

Built today

One game record in the backend; a full snapshot to each screen when it connects; live updates for scores, transactions and player scans; the TV and Pi 4 scoreboards.

The long-term goal

The Game record decides teams, claims, scores, evidence and phase: on the engine, or on the tablet in a standalone game, under the same rules. Delivery carries each fact by one route to the screens that may see it, live and after a reconnect; no screen keeps its own copy to decide from. Hidden facts never reach the wrong screen.

Kept by these parts of the candidate shapeGame recordDeliveryScreens
Broken by these design flaws today, the main one first
  • C1Every tablet keeps its own list of claimed tokens that only grows, so after a delete every connected tablet keeps refusing the token the engine has freed, and a tablet that lost its connection during a Reset All Scores, and did not reload, keeps the old claims when it reconnects.
  • C6What a tablet gets when it connects and what it gets live for the same fact come from different code, so a tablet that reloaded can differ from one that watched everything happen.
  • C9Tablets and phones work out claims, points and 'seen before' from their own copy of the rules, so the result screen shows a verdict the engine has not given.
  • C11One question has several answerers inside the engine or on one tablet, so 'is a video playing', 'which teams exist' and 'may I scan' get different answers in different places.
  • C2Equipment state reaches the panels on a different route from its health, so a panel can look calm while the engine knows the player is down.
  • C4A reconnecting tablet infers a game boundary from whatever arrives and may wipe its own history.
  • C5After a Reset System the broadcasts are wired before the saving step, so a later Reset All Scores sends every tablet and the TV the old transaction list, and cleared evidence comes back.
  • C7A reply does not say which request it answers, so a tablet's copy and the engine's record stop matching.
  • C12A tablet running a different pack than the engine is only written to a log.
  • C14Team Details reads the score from a place the tablet never fills, so it shows the wrong score while the scoreboard screen shows the right one.

In a standalone game one tablet holds the only copy, so tablets cannot disagree, but not every screen refreshes; two tablets are two games, one per game undecided. In the candidate shape the tablet runs the engine's Game record and Delivery, or its own engine kept equal by tests.

What the planned capabilities need from it (6)
  • The truth sweep (capability 28) and the GM scanner redesign (capability 27): a named decider for each fact the GM sees, applied to the ten service domains and the twenty-two accepted events; the redesign adds a new fact the GM writes, the game's phase.
  • Capture (capabilities 30 and 31): roster, notes, accusation and photos each need a decider, a place that survives a restart and a place in the reconnect catch-up. A note held only on a tablet is a copy, not a record.
  • The second game (capability 38) and players' phones (capability 35): hidden facts have a decider and a saved home, and the engine filters what each screen receives.
  • Service domains (capability 15): state and health travel together on one route, and every catch-up message carries every service. The same bug has come back four times.
  • Credentials (capability 12) and remote displays (capability 39): the engine knows which screens are connected and which record each may see.
  • Hot-apply (capability 16): every client is told that the pack changed.
Test cases from the shows (4); Answers the pass's questions 1, 5, 8, 9
  1. Delete a transaction on one tablet, then rescan its token on a second tablet with no reload: the scan is accepted.
  2. Drop a tablet's connection without reloading it, press Reset All Scores from another tablet, reconnect the first and rescan an old token: accepted.
  3. For each fact, build the live message and the connect-time entry from the same state: equal keys and values.
  4. Scan a token the engine will refuse: the result screen never says 'complete' and shows the engine's reason.

Every tap gets its answer, and the night can be told afterwards

Who
The GM at a token or button and the player at a token; after the show, the director who tells the night's story, the players it is told to, and the owner and designer
When
During play at every tap, and after the game when the night is reviewed
What they count on

At a tap on a token or a button they learn at once whether it counted, why not if it did not, or that it is held for later or must be tried again; nothing is silently lost or doubled. Afterwards the director tells the players the story of their night from what was captured during play, with the game and the texts they saw, not reconstructed; how that story reaches the players is the game's own business.

Built today

GM scans scored as transactions; player scans kept in the game record; an answer to every GM command; the post-game report and the session validator. Outside the engine, ALN's article writer, the Director Console, reads that report by hand.

The long-term goal

Every request gets one reply in the same form: which request it answers, the outcome, whether it is final, whether anything was stored. At the end, one record of the night is written from the facts decided and the roster, notes, accusation and photos captured during play, stamped with the game it ran and the texts the players saw. The tablet's report and the game's own telling both read it; for ALN that telling is the Director Console, a separate program, and the record leaves room for it to run inside the engine's Review stage later. Whether refused scans are recorded stays open.

Kept by these parts of the candidate shapeAnsweringThe night's record
Broken by these design flaws today, the main one first
  • C7A reply does not say which request it answers, whether it is final or whether anything was stored, so a device deletes scans the engine never kept and replays scans that can never succeed.
  • C4When the GM ends the game, a reconnecting tablet loses its history and the wall scoreboard goes blank.
  • C9The tablet shows its own verdict before the engine answers, so the GM can see 'Transaction Complete' for a scan the engine then refuses; in a standalone game a scan into a paused game shows complete and records nothing. Whether refused scans belong in the night's record is the owner's open question, not this flaw.
  • C11The downloaded report's two totals disagree.
  • C13The device list records a hardware scanner as a phone scanner, because the route it checks in on assumes 'player' when a device sends no type.
  • N1In a standalone game nothing records what players found. (proposed)

In a standalone game a refused scan shows Transaction Complete, a queued hardware-scanner scan shows as recorded, nothing records what players found, starting over deletes the finished game. In the candidate shape the tablet runs Answering, so a refusal shows; player scans reach the night's record if sent to it.

What the planned capabilities need from it (4)
  • Two planned capabilities touch the answer: held items (capability 14), where a held cue is an answer the GM sees, and the interaction primitives (capability 36), whose multi-tap actions are requests with the same reply shape.
  • Capture (capabilities 30 and 31), the session-bundle emitter (capability 32), the Review stage (capability 34) and the report pipeline move (capability 33): the engine writes a structured record of the night when the game ends, from facts the engine itself decided. Nothing writes that record today.
  • Photo capture (capability 31): a way to receive a picture, which the engine lacks, with the data kept on the kit and retention set per game by the owner.
  • Held items (capability 14): a held cue counts as an answer the GM can see, with a defined end for holds caused by a broken service.
Test cases from the shows (4); Answers the pass's questions 1, 8
  1. Send a batch of player scans while no game exists: the engine either refuses it so the device keeps the scans, or stores them. Never 'processed' with nothing kept.
  2. Send a batch, restart the engine, send the same batch again: the scans appear once.
  3. End a game and reconnect a tablet: it still shows its history, its scans and Download Report, and the wall scoreboard keeps the final scores until a new game starts.
  4. Press Skip twice quickly when the second press must fail: one success and one failure, each matched to its own press.

The room does what the designer cued and the GM asked

Who
The audience who see and hear the show; the GM at the Environment and Show Control sections
When
When the game says so, and whenever the GM presses a control
What they count on

The room does what the designer cued and what the GM asked; the GM sees it happen, or sees at once that it did not and what to press; a dead program comes back by itself before anyone notices.

Built today

Video on the TV, music, sounds, lights, the Bluetooth speaker and audio routing; cues and timelines; the health dashboard; items held while a service is down.

The long-term goal

The engine reaches each outside program through one interface and keeps a dated picture of it, checked against the program. One supervisor restarts a crashed program a few times, then shows a red row with a Restart button. A setting, in the profile as the candidate shape has it, says whether the engine starts or adopts each program. The Show runner keeps held cues and videos in one place.

Kept by these parts of the candidate shapeEquipmentShow runner
Broken by these design flaws today, the main one first
  • C2'Is it working' and 'what is it doing' reach the screens by different routes, so the music panel can keep a calm playing look while the engine knows the player is down.
  • C3The engine runs the video player, the music player and the sound server from a remembered picture it re-reads only in part and never dates, so the queue and the cues act on a belief that has drifted from the truth.
  • C5A Reset System leaves the music panel's feed out, so the panel is dead until a restart.
  • C10When the venue profile fails to load, six services that are installed read 'not installed', and the panel shows a calm green count.
  • C11'Is a video playing' has eight answers, and a paused video counts as not playing for four of them.

In a standalone game no outside program runs; the service panels are hidden on the tablet and each player device plays its own audio, so nothing here fails. In the candidate shape the tablet carries stand-ins for Equipment and Show runner that report every engine duty dormant: grey, never red.

What the planned capabilities need from it (6)
  • The supervisor (capability 9): each program gets one restart rule with bounded tries and then a red row, and the engine tells its own video or music player from a stranger's. Today four different restart shapes exist and the TV's browser has none.
  • The start-or-adopt setting (capability 10): a plain setting for whether the engine starts a program itself or adopts a running one, read once at start. The capability map gives it a file of its own; the candidate shape keeps it in the venue technician's profile. Step 3 settles which.
  • Self-heal (capability 11): the engine fixes a crashed program silently and raises a red row only for what it cannot fix.
  • Dormancy (capability 8): a not-installed-tonight verdict that sticks, silences the cues that depend on it and is never restarted, plus a decision about what a reset and a restart do to the GM's out-of-service latch.
  • Service domains (capability 15): no service keeps a private 'connected' note beside the shared health list, and one store for hold expiry instead of two.
  • Remote displays (capability 39) and the media bundle (capability 40): the engine knows whether a second screen is alive and whether the named video files exist on its disk.
Test cases from the shows (4); Answers the pass's questions 2, 3, 5
  1. Play a video: the engine reports 'started' within half a second every time, whichever order the player sends its signals.
  2. Kill the music player during play: the next push says stopped with no track, and the controls come back within about ten seconds with no reload.
  3. Stop the lighting system, then start it again: the lighting panel shows 'unavailable', then its scene tiles, with no reload.
  4. Pause a video, then run the queue: the paused video is not replaced, and a player who scans while a video is paused is not told the video was triggered.

Each lifecycle button does exactly what its name says

Who
The GM who starts, pauses, ends and resets games; the designer whose edit lands between games
When
At each lifecycle button, at an engine restart, and when a pack changes
What they count on

Each act does exactly what its name says: an ended game stays ended and no scan lands in it, the next game starts clean, a restart during a show loses nothing, a pause blocks scans, and a between-games edit never reaches the game in progress.

Built today

Create, start, pause and end a game; Reset System; a game restored after an engine restart; the game clock.

The long-term goal (open for step 3)

One Lifecycle moves a game between its moments and alone resets anything; engine start, Reset System and tests run the same steps. Its table says what each fact keeps, clears or carries, and every screen is told of the change. Pack changes land only between games; a new engine version only between events. Open: rows, and standing cues in setup.

Kept by these parts of the candidate shapeLifecycle
Broken by these design flaws today, the main one first
  • C4Nothing says what a game boundary clears or keeps, so an ended game returns after a restart and a once-only cue stays spent into the next game.
  • C5Starting the engine and rebuilding it after Reset System are two hand-kept lists of steps that differ, so a reset leaves out the music feed and brings old data back to the TV.
  • C1A tablet that lost its connection during a Reset All Scores, and did not reload, keeps its old claims when it reconnects.
  • C6A reload restores the saved game without firing the events a live change would.
  • C7A slow reset reports failure and the new game is skipped.

In a standalone game the tablet decides each moment (start, pause, end, a reload, a new day, a pack change) by its own steps; it opens a game it cannot name, pause or replace; player devices' lists and queues outlive every game. In the candidate shape it runs the engine's Lifecycle or its own, kept equal by tests.

What the planned capabilities need from it (8)
  • No capability exists mainly for this promise, but thirteen list it as a secondary promise (the eleven in the capabilities sheet's table, plus dormancy (capability 8) and the second game's modules (capability 38), which that sheet's section 3 places here); together they ask for one table of every fact against every moment.
  • Hot-apply (capability 16): refused while a game is active or paused; the setup, ended and no-game cases defined.
  • Self-heal (capability 11): the scanner's pack copy replaced at a game boundary, once per connection and engine version.
  • Dormancy (capability 8) and the preflight (capability 7): verdicts recomputed at reset, at game create and at restore, and the display's health not falsely red after a reset.
  • The session-bundle emitter (capability 32) and capture (capability 30): every fact that exists only on a tablet reaches the engine before the game ends.
  • The GM scanner redesign (capability 27): the new phase fact has a defined fate at pause, end and reset.
  • The second game (capability 38): hidden state persists across a restart.
  • Blue/green cutover (capability 44): the engine updates only between events; the test environment (capability 46): reset is one code path in tests and in production.
Test cases from the shows (4); Answers the pass's question 6
  1. End a game, restart the engine, scan a player token: refused with 'no game', and the engine reports no current game.
  2. Fire a once-only cue in game one, end it, create and start game two by the plain flow, trigger the cue again: it fires.
  3. Run Reset System with tablets connected: every tablet at once shows an empty game with no scores, claims or history.
  4. Scan three tokens, run Reset System, create and start a game, scan two, press Reset All Scores: the GM history and the TV evidence are empty.

The designer changes a value and plays it within minutes, and the live show never notices

Who
The game designer; the owner designing the second game; a new designer learning from the toy game
When
In the days before a show, on a laptop or on the show machine between games
What they count on

Build a game or change one value in it, publish and apply it, and play a round within minutes; the engine's own check refuses a bad pack at the desk; nothing they do can reach the live show or its records.

Built today

The config tool; a store for drafting and publishing packs.

The long-term goal

Edit, apply, play, repeat; the rules editor and applying a change to a running engine are built together. The Design studio talks to the engine only by message; the engine applies a change at a moment the Lifecycle allows, never mid-game, or refuses and says why. A preview engine is a second Engine instance, so it touches nothing live, and on a laptop the equipment reads as not installed. Where it runs is open.

Kept by these parts of the candidate shapeDesign studio
Broken by these design flaws today, the main one first
  • C8A second engine on the same machine kills some of the live engine's helpers (the sound system's watcher, the TV's browser and the music player); the design-tool plan warns that a preview engine would also write into the live engine's saved records and issue passes the live engine accepts.
  • C12Publishing a pack from the config tool tells the running engine nothing; the engine scores by the change only after a restart, while a reloaded TV already shows it.

In a standalone game no engine exists to rehearse against, so this promise has no gap here; a mid-game reload that takes a newer pack breaks "The designer's game is the game the whole kit plays". In the candidate shape the same Pack channel brings a published pack to the tablet, so the designer's publish is what it plays.

What the planned capabilities need from it (5)
  • The five Design pages (capability 17) and the draft and publish store (capability 4): edits touch a draft only, with the live engine's adopted version shown beside it.
  • Hot-apply (capability 16) and the design-iteration loop (capability 18): edit, publish, apply and play work in setup or after an end; a GM-scored trial needs a started game and so passes the start gate.
  • The preview engine (capability 20) and simulation profiles (capability 21): a second engine that cannot kill the live engine's programs, write into its records or mint passes it accepts.
  • Live verdict badges (capability 19): the same verdict function as the engine, run on the draft, labeled paper or live.
  • First-run state (capability 48), content tooling (capability 41) and the preset-load gate (capability 52) set no demand in the direction map's section 3.
Test cases from the shows (3); Answers the pass's questions 4, 6
  1. Publish a changed rating through the tool with the engine running: within a bounded time scoring, the token lists, the scoreboard and every tablet use it, or the engine answers that it will apply at the next window between games, or refuses and says why.
  2. Start a second engine on the same machine while a show runs: the first engine's video player, music player, the TV's browser and the sound system's watcher are untouched.
  3. Swap a pack mid-game and delete a transaction: scores equal what the original rules awarded, or the swap is refused during a game. Today a swap needs an engine restart, so the test swaps across a restart and keeps the game.

Each person sees their part and can do only their part

Who
The GM; the audience; a player with no account; designers and venue technicians; a stranger on the venue wifi
When
At every connection and every command
What they count on

The GM alone can start, pause, end and change scores, and the engine knows which tablet did it; the audience sees scores and exposed evidence and nothing else; a player sees the memory they scanned; designers and venue technicians reach their tools and only those; a stranger sees nothing.

Built today

The GM password and its login token; a watch-only pass for the Pi 4 scoreboard; anonymous player scans.

The long-term goal

Players never get accounts; the engine knows them only by device or session, and their data stays on the kit. Every connection shows a verified pass: kind, name, role, still there or not; each screen gets only what its role may see. One door checks every action once; only the GM moves the game, runs the show or changes scores.

Kept by these parts of the candidate shapeConnections and roles
Broken by these design flaws today, the main one first
  • C13A client's own word or a route's default decides whether a credential is asked for and what the station is called, so every connection gets everything and the engine cannot tell two tablets or a display from a GM.

In a standalone game no engine, pass or connection exists, so nothing fails; a tablet never set up for the network is called 001, known to nobody. In the candidate shape a Connections and roles stand-in knows one connection, itself, so the same door runs with no pass to check.

What the planned capabilities need from it (6)
  • Credentials on every connection (capability 12) and auth tiers (capability 13): every connection presents a verified pass, and the floor stays operator-only.
  • Players' phones (capability 35): passes issued without accounts, engine-side filtering of what each phone sees, and a measured capacity for how many phones one kit carries. No number exists.
  • The second game (capability 38): hidden state kept from every screen except the one entitled to it.
  • Remote displays (capability 39): a read-only display pass issued to each display connection, never written into the page's text, capped in number and counted against the profile's number of remote displays.
  • The GM scanner redesign (capability 27): the GM's four sections keep working while mid-game controls change.
  • Open-sourcing (capability 47) and the standing safety rule: the committed lighting token and admin password rotate before any outsider touches the system.
Test cases from the shows (4); Answers the pass's questions 7, 1
  1. Open a connection with no credential and a device type of 'scanner', then ask for the state: refused, nothing arrives.
  2. Ask the engine for the game state with no credential from the kit network: refused, or only what a player needs.
  3. Connect as a TV: it receives only the events its role needs, and what it receives on connecting holds only exposed evidence and team totals.
  4. Drop a tablet's network, or kill its browser without a clean close, then reload it mid-game: it reconnects under its own station name within five seconds.

The game fits the venue it is in tonight

Who
The venue technician; a GM running a game with no engine; a future operator with a second kit
When
In the days before an event, and on the night when the engine is absent by choice
What they count on

The venue technician states what the kit brings tonight, binds the pack's names to real instruments and gets one go or no-go. A game declared standalone knows it is standalone instead of showing a lost connection all night. A second kit or a new machine needs no change to engine code.

Built today

Venue profiles and "not installed tonight"; standalone modes on the GM tablet and the web player scanner; the blue and green machine method.

The long-term goal

The venue technician picks the pack and the profile by name, fills the profile, binds each pack name to an instrument and runs one preflight. What they count on

Built today

Health words healthy, down and dormant (grey) for each service on the health panel; a first, minimal preflight.

A change is proven before it reaches anyone, and the owner alone sends it to the show

Who
The developer changing the engine; the owner sending a version to the venue; the GM running a live show
When
Before every change reaches anyone, and on release day
What they count on

When every test passes, the show still runs for ALN and for the toy game; the owner decides when a new version goes to the venue, the machine carries one matching set, and the owner can swap back by replugging a machine; no test or rehearsal ever reaches a live show.

Built today

Unit, contract, integration and end-to-end tests; a rung-1 run with the real programs in CI, started by hand or by a change to its own files (the standing test rig was lost with the old container; step 2 rebuilds it); CI on every pull request to main; coverage that may not fall.

The long-term goal

Tests run in levels, from logic alone, through the real engine and its programs with stand-ins for the room's equipment, to a home test bed and the venue. Each test run, preview and second kit has its own names, so none reaches a live show. Shared shapes are written once and stand-ins are checked against the real thing; every change runs on two packs. The owner alone sends a checked machine, and can replug the old one.
