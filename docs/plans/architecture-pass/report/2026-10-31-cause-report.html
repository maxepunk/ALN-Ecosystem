What the engine must do

The engine runs shows for any game, not only ALN. This page starts from its ten jobs: what each job means for any game, what is built today, the long-term goal, and where today's design falls short. The problems from blue's shows appear as evidence and as test cases, not as the plan.

Standalone games, the game's record, the next step. Three questions about games with no engine, one about recording refused scans, and one about how much the test rig proves before design starts. Everything is at the end, each with a recommendation.

What the engine is for

The engine is the game-independent software that runs a show, and it is going toward an open-source show platform that designers who are not technical can use and technical people can extend.

A game designer builds a whole game as a pack in the design tool, never touching code or configuration files, and tunes it by changing a value, applying it and playing.

A venue installer fills in a profile of what the venue has, connects the pack's names to the real instruments in the room on one page, and runs one preflight that answers go or no-go with every line traceable.

A GM runs the show from four sections (Game Ops, Show Control, Environment, Game Admin), and the post-game report builds itself from what was captured during play.

A player taps tokens on a phone or a shared station with no account, no login, no app to install and no recorded identity unless the game designs an identification moment.

The business is one production, one kit and many games on it; a second kit or operator is not planned, but must stay cheap to start, so nothing in the engine may assume one kit, one pack or one production.

The standing rules: players' data stays on the kit; media never lives inside the pack; phones and hardware scanners stay equal; the engine holds no opinion about game design; red always means act now; the preflight states its own limits; and nothing deploys to the live machine until the owner says the new engine is ready.

A second, toy game stays in the repository for good, so every change is proven on two games and ALN stays one pack among others.

How to read a job

Each job says what it means for any game and who relies on it, then what is built today and the long-term goal. Under it sit the design flaws that keep it from being done well, each with a card.

A card tells one moment at a show, why it happens, and shows two diagrams. Today shows how the problem happens now. Candidate, not decided shows one way the design could remove it. Step 3 puts competing designs in front of you; nothing on the right is decided.

The design words

Each card ends with the reason restated in the words step 3 uses.

Module
Any part with an inside and an outside, from one function to the whole backend.
Interface
Everything another part must know to use it.
Seam
Where one part meets another, and where behaviour can be swapped.
Deep, shallow
A deep module does a lot behind a small interface. A shallow one makes its callers know almost as much as it does.
Locality
A fix in one place fixes it everywhere.
Leverage
One module serving many callers.

J1

Run any game from its pack

The pack describes the game: its tokens and their content, modes, scoring, groups, cues, the clock, what each screen shows. The engine plays whatever the pack says, and nothing in the engine assumes ALN. Every part of the kit runs the same version of the pack and can say which. The toy pack proves the engine is generic.

the game designer, whose pack is the whole gamethe GM and the players, who play what the pack declaresthe venue installer, who reads the pack's needsthe engine itself, which must not know ALN's words
Built today

Packs that hold tokens, modes, scoring, groups and cues; a gate that refuses a broken pack; a second, toy game kept beside ALN.

The long-term goal

A designer builds a complete game as a pack and never touches code or configuration; everything a designer iterates on is pack data, never firmware. The engine holds no opinion about game design. The pack names media by fingerprint; a bundle carries the files. A second game stays in the repository, and every change passes the two-pack run.

Where today's design falls short
  • C12The pack is read at scattered moments and no message says it changed, so the TV, the engine and a tablet can end up running different versions of one game.
  • C14The shape of the files the pack hands each device is kept by hand on both sides, so the hardware scanner cannot read the pack's own token file. (its card is under J10)
  • C15The videos and other media files the pack names are copied onto the show machine by hand, and nothing checks that every file the pack names is there. (its card is under J10)
  • N1The pack has no field that says a game runs without an engine, yet it declares a scoreboard, a clock and videos that an engine-less device cannot show. (proposed, question 23; it has no card)

In a standalone game Each device loads whatever pack copy its source holds; a tablet that reloads online mid-game adopts the newest published pack under the game in progress, no game record carries a pack stamp, and no player device shows a pack identity, so nothing compares the devices.

What the planned capabilities need from it (6)
  • The pack (capability 1) and the activation gate (capability 2): every part of the engine takes the game's tokens, rules, modes and cues from the pack, never from code; the engine refuses to start on a pack that fails the check and keeps the pack it adopted for as long as it runs.
  • The second game's modules (capability 38), interaction primitives (capability 36) and the entity-field migration (capability 37): scoring models, hidden facts, multi-tap actions and even the word for a score-holder come from the pack.
  • The media bundle (capability 40) and the ledger retirements (capability 50): the pack names its files by a fingerprint of their contents and carries no venue-hardware name.
  • Hot-apply (capability 16) and the pack channel (capability 3): one complete list of every part that holds a copy of the pack. No document has this list today.
  • Content tooling (capability 41): a replacement for Notion feeds the same pack through the same gate.
  • Hardware-scanner parity (capability 49): a mechanic that needs a fleet re-flash breaks the designer's loop, so everything a designer changes is pack data.
Test cases from the shows (4); Answers the pass's questions 6, 5, 8
  1. Edit one token's rating on disk with the engine running and no restart: the token list, the pack file and the points a scan awards all agree, and the file's fingerprint matches the one in the engine's own list of pack files.
  2. Connect a tablet that runs a different pack: the GM sees a mismatch on screen, or the tablet is refused.
  3. Run the group-completion test on the real pack and on the toy pack: it runs on both and does not skip.
  4. With no alternate pack chosen, compare the phone scanner's token file with the engine's own pack: they are the same version.
C12

Pack edits reach each part of the kit differently

20 problems foundQuestions 6, 5, 8

At a show A designer publishes a pack change and does not restart. The TV shows evidence by the new rules while the engine scores by the old. The file the engine serves no longer matches the hash in its own manifest, so a scanner that checks rejects it. A tablet running a different pack is only written to the log; nothing tells the GM. The publish tells the running engine nothing.

Why it happens The engine freezes one pack at start, yet its file endpoints serve whatever is on disk and its token endpoint re-reads every call. The TV, tablet and hardware scanner each load it at their own moment and keep it; the phone reads its own copy. No message says it changed.

In a standalone game each device loads whatever pack copy its source holds; the tablet can adopt a newer pack on a mid-game reload, and the game carries no pack stamp. No player device shows a pack identity, so nothing compares the devices.

In design terms The pack service should own one versioned pack view. Its interface leaks the disk: it checks a path against the frozen inventory, then serves live bytes, and the token service re-reads the file around it, so frozen and live state cross the seam. No locality.

J2

Keep one true record of the game, and show it truly everywhere

Teams, scores, claimed tokens, exposed evidence and the game's phase are each decided in one place: the engine in a networked game, the GM tablet in a standalone one. Every screen (GM tablets, the TV and other scoreboards, player devices) shows that record, also after reconnecting. J2 makes a screen right; J9 makes it say so when it cannot be.

the GM, whose every decision must show the same on every tabletthe audience, who read the public scoreboardsplayers, in future games with hidden factsthe designer, whose hidden facts must have one home
Built today

One game record in the backend; a full snapshot to each screen when it connects; live updates for scores, transactions and player scans; the TV and Pi 4 scoreboards.

The long-term goal

The engine decides the scores in a networked game and the GM tablet in a standalone one, with the engine's pure rule functions as the surface both must match. Games with hidden facts need the engine to filter what each screen receives; filtering on the screen is fatal for them. How copies are kept correct is step 3's work.

Where today's design falls short
  • C1Every tablet keeps its own list of claimed tokens that only grows, so after a delete every connected tablet keeps refusing the token the engine has freed, and a tablet that was offline during a Reset All Scores keeps the old claims when it reconnects.
  • C6What a tablet gets when it connects and what it gets live for the same fact come from different code, so a tablet that reloaded can differ from one that watched everything happen.
  • C9Tablets and phones work out claims, points and 'seen before' from their own copy of the rules, so the result screen shows a verdict the engine has not given.
  • C11One question has several answerers inside one part of the engine or one tablet, so 'is a video playing', 'which teams exist' and 'may I scan' get different answers in different places.
  • C2Equipment state reaches the panels on a different route from its health, so a panel can look calm while the engine knows the player is down. (its card is under J4)
  • C4A reconnecting tablet infers a game boundary from whatever arrives and may wipe its own history. (its card is under J5)
  • C5After a Reset System the broadcasts are wired before the saving step, so a later Reset All Scores sends every tablet and the TV the old transaction list, and cleared evidence comes back. (its card is under J5)
  • C7A reply does not say which request it answers, so a tablet's copy and the engine's record part ways. (its card is under J3)
  • C12A tablet running a different pack than the engine is only written to a log. (its card is under J1)
  • C14Team Details reads the score from a place the tablet never fills, so it shows the wrong score while the scoreboard screen shows the right one. (its card is under J10)

In a standalone game One GM tablet holds the only copy, so tablets cannot drift, but it keeps a claim list beside its transactions and refreshes screens only on wired events. Two standalone tablets are two separate games, and whether a standalone game means one tablet is undecided.

What the planned capabilities need from it (6)
  • The truth sweep (capability 28) and the GM scanner redesign (capability 27): a named decider for each fact the GM sees, applied to the ten service domains and the twenty-two accepted events; the redesign adds a new fact the GM writes, the game's phase.
  • Capture (capabilities 30 and 31): roster, notes, accusation and photos each need a decider, a place that survives a restart and a place in the reconnect catch-up. A note held only on a tablet is a copy, not a record.
  • The second game (capability 38) and players' phones (capability 35): hidden facts have a decider and a saved home, and the engine filters what each screen receives.
  • Service domains (capability 15): state and health travel together on one route, and every catch-up message carries every service. The same bug has come back four times.
  • Credentials (capability 12) and remote displays (capability 39): the engine knows which screens are connected and which record each may see.
  • Hot-apply (capability 16): every client is told that the pack changed.
Test cases from the shows (4); Answers the pass's questions 1, 5, 8, 9

  2. Drop a tablet's connection without reloading it, press Reset All Scores from another tablet, reconnect the first and rescan an old token: accepted.

C1

Each tablet keeps its own list of claimed tokens

19 problems foundQuestions 1, 8Clue from blue: fix W1

At a show A GM deletes a transaction. The engine frees the token, but every tablet that stays connected keeps refusing it as already scanned until someone reloads. A tablet that drops its connection during Reset All Scores comes back still holding the old claims, and refuses tokens the engine has cleared.

Why it happens The engine decides which tokens are claimed, but each tablet holds a copy that only grows: added on scan, merged on reconnect, removed only when a scan fails. The engine's own messages also skip facts changed by another path, such as a team created by a scan.

In a standalone game one GM tablet holds the only copy, so tablets cannot drift, but it keeps a claim list beside its transactions and refreshes screens only on wired events. Two standalone tablets are two separate games; one tablet per game is undecided.

In design terms The engine's transaction service should own the claimed-token fact; tablets should hold only its copy. Today the tablet's networked storage module updates its copy through one hand-wired handler per message, so the seam leaks: any path without a handler leaves the copy stale. No locality.

Reconnect snapshot and live update are built apart

At a show A GM reloads a tablet mid-game. The History badge stays hidden until the next scan, and a rebuilt admin panel can show the clock as --:-- until the next change. The device list flips between everyone ever and connected only, depending on which message came last.

Why it happens For each fact the engine has one builder for the connect snapshot and another for the live message, and six places send the snapshot with hand-picked arguments. The tablet applies each by a different rule, and the restore path fires none of the events screens listen for.

J3

Answer every action, and remember what happened

Every scan and every GM command gets one clear answer: done, refused and why, or try again. The game keeps a record of what was done, by GMs and by players, so the night can be reviewed, reported and captured afterwards. The two halves have different flaws and different tests; a question at the end of this page asks whether they are two jobs.

the GM, who needs a plain answer to every tapplayers, whose devices need to know whether a scan was keptthe owner, who reviews and reports the nightthe designer, whose Review stage tells the story of the night
Built today

GM scans scored as transactions; player scans kept in the game record; an answer to every GM command; the post-game report and the session validator.

The long-term goal

The post-game report builds itself from material captured during play instead of being reconstructed afterwards. Roster, notes, accusation and later photos are captured into the engine's own record, and a structured record of the night is written when the game ends. Whether every scan attempt's outcome is recorded is still the owner's open question.

Where today's design falls short
  • C7A reply does not say which request it answers, whether it is final or whether anything was stored, so a device deletes scans the engine never kept and replays scans that can never succeed.
  • C4When the GM ends the game, a reconnecting tablet loses its history and the wall scoreboard goes blank. (its card is under J5)
  • C9The tablet shows its own verdict before the engine answers, so the GM can see 'Transaction Complete' for a scan the engine then refuses; in a standalone game a scan into a paused game shows complete and records nothing. Whether refused scans belong in the night's record is the owner's open question, not this flaw. (its card is under J2)
  • C11The downloaded report's two totals disagree. (its card is under J2)
  • C13The device list records a hardware scanner as a phone scanner, because the route it checks in on assumes 'player' when a device sends no type. (its card is under J7)
  • N1In a standalone game nothing records what players found. (proposed, question 23; it has no card)

In a standalone game No network reply exists, but each device's own store answers and the caller ignores it: a refused scan still shows Transaction Complete, and the hardware scanner shows a queued scan as recorded. Nothing records what players found, and the only way to start over deletes the finished game's record.

What the planned capabilities need from it (4)
  • No planned capability concerns the answer to a scan or a GM command; that half of J3 is the engine's existing core, which the capability list does not grade.
  • Capture (capabilities 30 and 31), the session-bundle emitter (capability 32), the Review stage (capability 34) and the report pipeline move (capability 33): the engine writes a structured record of the night when the game ends, from facts the engine itself decided. Nothing writes that record today.
  • Photo capture (capability 31): a way to receive a picture, which the engine lacks, with the data kept on the kit and retention set per game by the owner.
  • Held items (capability 14): a held cue counts as an answer the GM can see, with a defined end for holds caused by a broken service.
Test cases from the shows (4); Answers the pass's questions 1, 8
  1. Send a batch of player scans while no game exists: the engine either refuses it so the device keeps the scans, or stores them. Never 'processed' with nothing kept.
  2. Send a batch, restart the engine, send the same batch again: the scans appear once.
  3. End a game and reconnect a tablet: it still shows its history, its scans and Download Report, and the wall scoreboard keeps the final scores until a new game starts.
  4. Press Skip twice quickly when the second press must fail: one success and one failure, each matched to its own press.
C7

A reply never says which request, final or stored

18 problems foundQuestions 1, 8

At a show Player devices upload their queued scans while no game exists. The engine answers processed, stores nothing, and both devices delete the scans. A GM's scan that can never succeed comes back as a generic error, so the tablet replays it at every reconnect with a toast each time. A slow Reset System reports failure and the new game is skipped.

Why it happens The engine answers before or without the write that makes the fact true, calls an unstored batch processed, returns some failures as a typed result and others as a generic error, and acknowledges commands by action name alone. Each device then decides for itself what a reply means.

In a standalone game no network reply exists, but each device's own store answers and the caller ignores it: a refused scan still shows Transaction Complete, and the ESP32 shows a queued scan as recorded. Each device's record step must return the same result words.

In design terms One commit module in the engine should own each request's outcome: which request, final or not, stored or not. That interface is split between typed results, generic errors and action-name acks, so the seam leaks and three clients each keep their own reply rule.

  • B-22 / D-13 / D-14 A batch arriving with no game is answered processed but stored nowhere; both player devices delete it.
  • B-21 / D-15 A batch retried after an engine restart is stored twice; processed batches are remembered in memory for an hour.
  • B-69 / C-35 A system reset slower than the tablet's five-second wait reports failure, and the follow-on create session is skipped.
  • B-65 / C-33 A command ack carries only action, success and message, so two presses of one action resolve on the first reply.
  • D-12 One failed batch is dropped by the web scanner on any 4xx but kept forever by the ESP32.

J4

Run the show's equipment, and know its true state

Video, music, sound, lights, displays and speakers do what the GM and the cues ask. The engine knows whether each is working, down, or not installed tonight, restarts what it can and lets the GM control the rest, by hand or by cue. The outside programs it drives decide what they are doing; the engine reads them, never guesses.

the GM, who runs the venue from the Environment and Show Control sectionsthe audience, who watch the TV and hear the speakersthe venue installer, whose profile says what is installed tonightplayers, whose video tokens start a video
Built today

Video on the TV, music, sounds, lights, the Bluetooth speaker and audio routing; cues and timelines; the health dashboard; items held while a service is down.

The long-term goal

Three health words: healthy, down and dormant; dormant sticks, with two doors: not installed tonight, or taken out of service by the GM. One supervisor restarts a crashed program a few times, then shows a red row with a Restart button. A host file says whether the engine starts or adopts a program. An outside program decides what it does.

Where today's design falls short
  • C2'Is it working' and 'what is it doing' reach the screens by different routes, so the music panel can keep a calm playing look while the engine knows the player is down.
  • C3The engine runs the video player, the music player and the sound server from a remembered picture it re-reads only in part and never dates, so the queue and the cues act on a belief that has drifted from the truth.
  • C5A Reset System leaves the music panel's feed out, so the panel is dead until a restart. (its card is under J5)
  • C10When the venue profile fails to load, six services that are installed read 'not installed', and the panel shows a calm green count. (its card is under J9)
  • C11'Is a video playing' has eight answers, and a paused video counts as not playing for four of them. (its card is under J2)

In a standalone game No venue service runs and no outside program is driven: the service panels are hidden on the tablet and each player device plays its own audio. These two flaws produce no fault with no engine.

What the planned capabilities need from it (6)

Standalone games

Your decision 22 adds a requirement: a whole game can run with no engine, the GM tablet and the player scanners each working alone, as a choice the game declares and never mixed. In a standalone game the GM tablet decides everything about the game (used tokens, scores, teams, the game's start, pause and end) from its own hand-kept copies of the engine's rules. Each player scanner decides only whether a token is known, and the phone also whether it has seen that token before.

Four design flaws cause no problem in a standalone game, four apply in part and eight in full. Each job and each card says what changes. The two biggest gaps: nothing says that a game is standalone, and every device shows the missing engine as a failure ("Offline" on the phone all night, red "DISCONNECTED" on the ESP32, "VIDEO UNAVAILABLE, Rescan later" for a video that can never play).
