What the engine is for

The engine is the game-independent software that runs a show, and it is going toward an open-source show platform that designers who are not technical can use and technical people can extend.

A game designer builds a whole game as a pack in the design tool, never touching code or configuration files, and tunes it by changing a value, applying it and playing.

A venue technician fills in a profile of what the venue has, connects the pack's names to the real instruments in the room on one page, and runs one preflight that answers go or no-go with every line traceable.

A GM runs the show from four sections (Game Ops, Show Control, Environment, Game Admin), and the post-game report builds itself from what was captured during play.

A player taps tokens on a phone or a shared station with no account, no login, no app to install and no recorded identity unless the game designs an identification moment.

The business is one production, one kit and many games on it; a second kit or operator is not planned, but must stay cheap to start, so nothing in the engine may assume one kit, one pack or one production.

The standing rules: players' data stays on the kit; media never lives inside the pack; phones and hardware scanners stay equal; the engine holds no opinion about game design; red always means act now; the preflight states its own limits; and nothing deploys to the live machine until the owner says the new engine is ready.

A second, toy game stays in the repository for good, so every change is proven on two games and ALN stays one pack among others.

How the promises fit together

You asked how the ten promises relate, so that the design keeps all of them at once and stays easy to keep building. This section answers at two levels: the engine and the devices it talks to (the tablets, the scanners, the scoreboards, the config tool), and the smaller parts inside them. It shows how the promises depend on each other, where today's code keeps or breaks them, and one candidate shape for the whole engine. Every card further down now follows that shape, and each says what changed.

What happens at every tap. In a networked game, four promises meet at every tap. The designer's pack sets the rules. The game's record, the same on every screen, is what the tap is judged against. The answer comes back to the GM or the player, and an accepted tap joins the record. The roles say who tapped and who may see the result.

The engine's own machinery. The equipment reports what it is doing. The verdict turns that, and the venue technician's profile, into go, no-go or "not installed tonight". The life of a game says when to check again and what to reset, which feeds back into the equipment. The profile feeds this loop from outside. In today's code the loop is also a chain of seven backend modules that depend on each other in a circle, with the game's record caught inside it.

Two promises reach the show from outside. The designer's week and the developer's proof reach a live show only through the pack and through each engine having its own names, so a test or a preview never touches the live show.

How each promise is kept. Most promises are kept by one or two parts of their own. Two are kept by a quality every part must have, together with a few parts of their own: "Anyone reading a screen can act on it without a second question" by a label on every fact (how sure, how old), and "A change is proven before it reaches anyone, and the owner alone sends it to the show" by tests every part passes. "Each lifecycle button does exactly what its name says" rests on a table of moments (setup, start, pause, end, reset, restart, the next game) that most other promises read.

Where the promises' work sits in today's code: 13 tangles

A tangle is one piece of work spread over many places with nothing joining them, one place doing many kinds of work, or a dependency running the wrong way. Each causes some of the design flaws below.

  • The pack is held in nine places and nothing joins them. The engine's frozen snapshot and the live disk bytes under it, the tablet's cache, the phone's own file, the hardware scanner's card, the TV's own fetch, the design tool's working copy, the sync script's output, two nested copies and media in three folders. No message says the pack changed, so after a publish the TV, the engine and a tablet can run three versions of one game. Flaws: C12 C14 N1
  • The game's facts are decided once and re-decided in five parts. The tablet marks a token claimed, computes the points and shows complete before the engine answers; the phone and the hardware scanner decide whether a token exists; the TV decides which modes count as evidence; a standalone tablet decides everything from a hand-kept copy of the rules. Eighteen rules are re-decided on clients, and for five of them nothing keeps the copies equal. Flaws: C1 C9 C11 C6
  • One fact, two builders; state and health on two routes. For seven facts the snapshot a tablet gets on connect and the live message for the same fact are built by different code. A service's state and its health arrive as separate pushes. No fact carries an age, and no screen reads the ages that exist. Flaws: C6 C2 C10
  • Three doors, three reply shapes, acknowledgements matched by name. A player scan, a GM scan and a GM command each get a differently shaped reply. The tablet matches an acknowledgement to a request by the action name alone, so two quick presses cannot be told apart. The phone and the hardware scanner read the same status differently. Flaws: C7
  • Who is connected has no home. The engine asks a pass only of a connection that calls itself a GM; the TV calls itself a GM; the whole game state can be fetched with no pass; a health check registers a device and files a hardware scanner as a phone; the design tool runs a second login against the same password; five clients each draw their own reachability word. A candidate shape: 17 parts

Most of today's problems sit in work several promises depend on: getting a fact onto a screen, answering a request, knowing who is connected, the boundary between one game and the next. Each is done several times today, in different places. In the candidate shape each such piece of work becomes one part, and that part's card says how the work is done today. The parts that are already well built today (the game rules, the verdict, the health words) are the pattern for the rest.

C. "The game fits the venue it is in tonight" now says a standalone game is a choice the game declares, never mixed.

These are your decision 22's words, and the map supports them: whether a game is standalone is an input every rule takes, and fitting the game to tonight's setup is where it is declared. Where the declaration is kept is a step-3 choice: the venue profile, the pack, the game's record or each device. The map favours the venue profile: a profile that lists no engine equipment already makes every engine duty read "not installed tonight", which is what a standalone game needs.

Question 23 is folded in here: is "nothing declares a game standalone" a design flaw of its own, with its own rule? If not, its eight problems join C9, C11 and C14 and the ESP32 parity list. On the map it lands on the pack channel and venue fit, or on the game's record if the declaration is kept there; the answer to 23 does not move it.

Recommendation: agree. On question 23, yes: three other rules (game boundaries, one answer per question, how old a copy is) need the declared fact as an input, and would otherwise each invent their own answer.

D. Split "Every tap gets its answer, and the night can be told afterwards" into two promises?

Answering happens at the tap, for the GM or the player. The night's record is for you and the designer after the show: what is captured during play, the session bundle, the review. The map confirms the two share no part. Answering sits in the middle of the engine, and six promises depend on it. The night's record is written once, at the end of a game, and read by the designer's review. The parts are the same either way.

Keeping one promise means the GM at the tap and you after the show look for what they count on in one sentence. Splitting makes a promise that no design flaw mainly breaks yet, because the night's record is mostly planned work.

Recommendation: split, into "Every tap gets its answer" and "The night can be told afterwards". The two halves serve two people at two moments and share no part, so two sentences let each find theirs.

E. Keep "The game fits the venue it is in tonight" as one promise, rather than split it?

Fitting the game to tonight's venue is one part with an output of its own: the venue technician's profile, which says what the kit brings, binds each of the pack's names to a real instrument and says whether the engine starts each program; the verdict turns it into one go or no-go. The map found that "run in any setup" has no part of its own. Running with no engine is a switch every rule takes, declared as part of fitting the game. Running on another kit is each engine knowing its own names, the same part that keeps a test from touching the live show. So a separate promise for it would own nothing.

Two titles fit the map. "The game fits the venue, whatever the setup" keeps standalone games and other kits in the title. "The game fits the venue it is in tonight" names the venue technician's moment, and leaves standalone games and other kits to the promise's sentences. The cost of not splitting: the GM running a standalone game and a future operator with another kit are named in a sentence, not in a title.

Recommendation: one promise, titled "The game fits the venue it is in tonight", with standalone games kept in its first sentences. This replaces the earlier recommendation to split.

Recommendation changed by the map

F. "A change is proven before it reaches anyone, and the owner alone sends it to the show": one promise, two, or release day moved?

The map separates this promise's two moments. Before every change, the developer's tests pass and show the show still works for ALN and for the toy game; that is a quality every part must have, and checking that joined parts agree is part of it. On release day, you decide when a new version goes to the venue and can swap back by replugging a machine; that is one small part, the release, which checks that everything installed matches.

  1. Keep one promise covering both moments, as the title does.
  2. Split release day into a promise of its own. It gives your release day its own sentence, kept by one small part.
  3. File release day with the venue technician's deploy work, under "The game fits the venue it is in tonight". The check of what is installed sits where the venue technician works, but your decision to send a version moves away from the developer's proof.
  4. Go back to the earlier wording, about tests alone. The parts fit that too; the cost is that your release day stays unnamed.

Question 4 covers keeping tests away from the live show (C8). For the other three flaws under this promise no question of the nine fits: question 5 is only the nearest for the names two parts share (C14), and the installed pieces (C15) and tests that prove the real path (C16) have none. This replaces question 18.

Recommendation: one promise, as the title has it, and give the proof and the release a question each in the pass. Both moments exist so that nothing reaches the live show unproven, or before you say so. The earlier split into "prove it works" and "keep every part in step" does not match the map, because keeping joined parts in step is part of the proof.

About the game's record

19. When a GM scan is refused, does the game keep a record of the attempt?

For example, a token already used, or no game running. Today only the tablet that scanned hears about it. On the map this decides whether the game's record keeps refused attempts, or only the answer goes back to the tablet. Yes means the record gains a kind of fact that no screen shows yet. No means a refused attempt is recorded nowhere, as today: only the tablet that scanned hears about it, and the night's record cannot tell it.

Recommendation: yes. Record every attempt and its answer, and show it nowhere until a feature needs it.
